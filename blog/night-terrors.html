Night Terrors: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Night terrors are a disorder of arousal from deep, slow-wave NREM sleep — not a dream — which is why they usually happen in the first few hours of the night and why the person rarely remembers them, unlike nightmares, which occur during REM sleep and are often recalled in vivid detail.
  • A clinical review in Neurologic Clinics and the NCBI Bookshelf's overview of sleep terrors put prevalence at roughly 1% to 6.5% in children, dropping to around 1–2% in adults, with most children outgrowing the episodes by adolescence.
  • Known triggers include sleep deprivation, fever, stress, alcohol, and conditions that fragment deep sleep — including obstructive sleep apnea and periodic limb movement disorder — meaning frequent episodes, especially in adults, are often worth mentioning to a doctor rather than dismissed as "just stress."

A night terror looks alarming: someone sits up suddenly, sometimes screaming, with a racing heart and open eyes that don't seem to focus on anything — but they aren't awake in any meaningful sense, and trying to comfort them mid-episode usually doesn't register. That mismatch, between how dramatic it looks and how little the person experiencing it recalls, is the clearest clue to what's actually happening. Sleep researchers classify night terrors as a parasomnia and, more specifically, a disorder of arousal — the brain gets partially and abruptly activated out of deep sleep without fully waking up.

That distinction matters because it separates night terrors from nightmares in a concrete, measurable way. Nightmares occur during REM sleep, which is concentrated in the second half of the night, and because REM is closely tied to dreaming and to the kind of memory consolidation that makes dream content stick, people usually remember them. Night terrors instead arise out of stage 3–4 NREM sleep — the deepest, slowest-wave stage — which dominates the first third or so of a night's sleep. Since there's no dream narrative being interrupted and the arousal itself is incomplete, there's typically nothing to recall the next morning.

What pushes someone into an episode isn't fully settled, but several triggers show up consistently across the clinical literature. Sleep deprivation is one of the best documented: missing sleep increases the amount of slow-wave sleep the brain generates on the next opportunity to rebound, which in turn raises the odds of an arousal-related parasomnia. Fever does something similar by deepening slow-wave sleep. Genetics plays a role too — night terrors run in families more than chance would predict. In adults, they're also associated with sleep disorders that repeatedly interrupt deep sleep, such as obstructive sleep apnea, as well as with PTSD and other mood disorders, which is part of why persistent adult-onset night terrors are usually treated as worth a clinical workup rather than a personality quirk.

For most children, the practical reality is reassuring: episodes tend to fade on their own by the early teenage years, and the priority for parents is safety — keeping the bedroom free of hazards in case of sleepwalking-like movement — rather than trying to wake or comfort the child mid-episode. For anyone, child or adult, protecting regular, sufficient sleep is the most consistent way to reduce how deep and disrupted that early slow-wave sleep gets in the first place, which is exactly the stage where these episodes originate.

Further reading: Night Terrors: Causes, Treatment, and Prevention (Sleep Foundation) and Night Terrors (NCBI Bookshelf, StatPearls).
