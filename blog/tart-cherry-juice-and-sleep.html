Tart Cherry Juice and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Montmorency tart cherries naturally contain melatonin, tryptophan, and serotonin, and their anthocyanins may help keep more tryptophan available for the body to convert into sleep-related compounds.
  • A pilot study in the Journal of Medicinal Food found that older adults with chronic insomnia who drank tart cherry juice twice daily for two weeks fell asleep for about 17 more minutes of uninterrupted sleep and scored lower on the Insomnia Severity Index than during a placebo period.
  • The evidence is still early — trials are small, short, and often funded by juice manufacturers — so researchers describe tart cherry juice as a modest, food-based habit rather than a proven insomnia treatment.

Tart cherry juice has built a reputation as a natural sleep aid, and unlike most fruit, cherries genuinely do contain compounds tied to sleep regulation. Montmorency tart cherries — the variety used in nearly all the research — carry measurable amounts of melatonin, along with tryptophan and serotonin, the amino acid and neurotransmitter the body uses to build melatonin in the first place. Some researchers also point to a second mechanism: the anthocyanins that give tart cherries their color may inhibit an enzyme that normally breaks tryptophan down, leaving more of it available for the body's own sleep chemistry rather than adding melatonin directly.

The best-known human trial is a small pilot study by Pigeon and colleagues, published in the Journal of Medicinal Food in 2010. Fifteen older adults with chronic insomnia drank 8 ounces of tart cherry juice in the morning and evening for two weeks, then switched to a matched placebo juice for two weeks, with a washout period in between. On cherry juice, participants spent about 17 fewer minutes awake after initially falling asleep, had measurably better sleep efficiency, and reported significant drops in insomnia severity compared with the placebo period.

That's a genuinely interesting result, but it's worth sizing up correctly: 15 participants is a small sample, the trial ran for only two weeks per arm, and it hasn't been replicated at scale. A more recent systematic review of the tart cherry literature reached a similar conclusion — the direction of the evidence is promising, but the overall body of research remains limited, and several of the underlying studies were funded by cherry juice companies. Sleep researchers generally treat tart cherry juice as a low-risk, food-based habit worth trying rather than a substitute for treatments with stronger evidence, like cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia.

If you want to try it, the studied dose is straightforward: roughly one cup in the morning and one cup an hour or so before bed, using unsweetened tart (not sweet) cherry juice, since sweet cherry varieties haven't shown the same melatonin content. Just factor in the sugar and calories of drinking juice daily, and don't expect it to outperform the basics — consistent sleep and wake times still matter more than any single food.

Further reading: Effects of a Tart Cherry Juice Beverage on the Sleep of Older Adults with Insomnia: A Pilot Study (Pigeon et al., Journal of Medicinal Food, via PubMed) and Does Tart Cherry Juice Help You Sleep? (Sleep Foundation).
