Sleep and Your Immune System: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • In a 2015 study published in the journal Sleep, healthy adults whose sleep was tracked with actigraphy before controlled exposure to a cold virus were more than four times as likely to develop a cold if they'd slept less than 6 hours a night, compared with those sleeping 7 or more.
  • Sleep loss is linked to reduced natural killer cell activity, suppressed interleukin-2 production, and higher circulating levels of inflammatory cytokines — changes that blunt the immune system's ability to detect and respond to pathogens.
  • A 2023 meta-analysis found that people getting under 6 hours of sleep around the time of vaccination produced a meaningfully weaker antibody response than well-rested people, across both flu and hepatitis vaccine studies.

"Get some rest, you'll fight it off faster" is one of those pieces of folk advice that turns out to hold up remarkably well under actual scientific scrutiny. Sleep and immune function are tightly linked, and the connection runs in both directions: illness changes how we sleep, and how we sleep changes how well our immune system can respond in the first place.

The clearest evidence comes from a 2015 study led by Aric Prather and published in the journal Sleep. Researchers tracked participants' sleep for a week using wrist actigraphy, then deliberately exposed them to a rhinovirus — the common cold — under quarantine conditions and monitored who actually developed symptoms. People who'd averaged fewer than 5 hours of sleep a night were about 4.5 times more likely to catch a cold than those sleeping 7 hours or more; the 5-to-6-hour group fared only slightly better, at roughly 4.2 times the risk. Because sleep was measured objectively before exposure, rather than self-reported after the fact, the study offered unusually strong evidence that short sleep itself — not just stress or other confounding habits — was driving the difference.

The mechanisms behind this are reasonably well mapped. Sleep loss has repeatedly been shown to reduce the activity of natural killer cells, an early-response immune cell type that targets virus-infected cells directly, and to suppress interleukin-2, a signaling protein that helps coordinate the broader immune response. At the same time, insufficient sleep tends to raise circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which in short bursts are a normal part of fighting infection but become counterproductive when chronically elevated. Sleep, in other words, isn't just downtime for the immune system — deep sleep in particular appears to be when several of these coordinating processes actually happen.

This extends to how well the immune system learns, not just how well it defends in the moment. A 2023 meta-analysis pooling data across influenza and hepatitis vaccine trials found that people who slept less than 6 hours around the time of vaccination developed a measurably weaker antibody response than those who slept longer — meaning the same vaccine may offer less durable protection to someone who's chronically underslept. None of this means sleep is a cure for illness, or that eight perfect hours guarantees immunity. But of all the "boost your immune system" advice out there, consistent sleep is one of the few backed by controlled, repeated evidence.

Further reading: Behaviorally Assessed Sleep and Susceptibility to the Common Cold (Sleep) and A Meta-Analysis of the Associations Between Insufficient Sleep Duration and Antibody Response to Vaccination (Current Biology).
