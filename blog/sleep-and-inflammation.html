Sleep and Inflammation: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2016 meta-analysis pooling 72 studies and over 50,000 participants found that poor sleep quality and long sleep duration were linked to higher CRP and IL-6, two markers of systemic inflammation — but short sleep duration alone showed a much weaker link.
  • That same analysis found no reliable effect from lab-induced sleep deprivation, which conflicted with some earlier single studies and suggested the real-world, chronic picture doesn't map neatly onto short lab experiments.
  • A 2025 updated meta-analysis found a single night of total or partial sleep loss didn't move inflammatory markers, but three or more consecutive nights of partial deprivation did raise IL-6 — pointing to duration as the missing variable.

It's become a common claim that a single bad night of sleep sends inflammation markers soaring. The actual research is more cautious, and more interesting, than that. The most comprehensive look at the question is a 2016 meta-analysis published in Biological Psychiatry by Michael Irwin and colleagues, which pooled data from 72 studies covering more than 50,000 people. It found that sleep disturbance — things like poor sleep quality and insomnia symptoms — and long sleep duration were both associated with higher levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), two of the most widely used blood markers of systemic inflammation. Short sleep duration on its own showed a much weaker association with CRP, and none of the three factors (disturbance, duration, or short sleep) reliably moved TNF-alpha, a third inflammatory marker.

What stood out in that same analysis is what didn't hold up: when researchers looked specifically at experimental sleep deprivation studies — people kept awake or sleep-restricted in a lab for one or several nights — there was no consistent link to CRP, IL-6, or TNF-alpha. That's a meaningful gap between the tidy, chronic associations seen in long-running cohort studies and what shows up when you deprive someone of sleep on purpose for a short, controlled stretch.

A 2025 update in the Journal of Sleep Research, led by Andrea Ballesio, helps explain that gap. Reviewing the experimental literature again, the authors found that a single night of total or partial sleep loss generally didn't budge inflammatory markers — but three or more consecutive nights of partial deprivation, with sleep cut to roughly four and a half hours, were associated with a measurable rise in IL-6. In other words, duration seems to be the variable that matters: one rough night is unlikely to do much, but a stretch of several short nights in a row is a different story.

CRP and IL-6 aren't just abstract lab values — both are established risk markers for cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes, which is why researchers keep returning to this question. The honest summary is that the strongest, most consistent evidence links inflammation to sustained poor sleep quality or long-term short sleep, not to any single night. It's one more reason consistency across a week tends to matter more than any individual night's sleep.

Further reading: Sleep Disturbance, Sleep Duration, and Inflammation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (Biological Psychiatry, via PubMed) and Effects of Experimental Sleep Deprivation on Peripheral Inflammation: An Updated Meta-Analysis (Journal of Sleep Research, via NCBI).
