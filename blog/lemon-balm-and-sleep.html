Lemon Balm for Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • In a 2024 double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of 30 adults, a standardized lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) extract lowered Insomnia Severity Index scores by 2.9 points versus placebo (p = 0.003), and 87% of participants rated their sleep improved, versus 30% on placebo.
  • Using wearable sleep tracking, the same trial recorded about 15% more slow-wave sleep and about 10% less REM sleep on the active extract compared with placebo.
  • The evidence base is still small and short-term, and the trial's lead author is also the scientific director of the company selling the tested extract — a conflict of interest worth weighing before treating lemon balm as a proven insomnia fix.

Lemon balm (Melissa officinalis) is a lemon-scented member of the mint family with a long history in folk medicine as a calming herb, usually brewed as tea or paired with valerian root in traditional "nerve tonic" blends. Separating its own effect from the sedating herbs it's often combined with has made it a harder herb to study cleanly than more extensively researched options like valerian or chamomile.

The proposed mechanism centers on rosmarinic acid, a compound in lemon balm thought to modestly inhibit GABA transaminase, an enzyme that breaks down the calming neurotransmitter GABA — in theory leaving more of it available in the brain. This remains a hypothesis drawn mostly from preclinical work rather than a confirmed mechanism in humans.

The clearest recent human evidence comes from a 2024 trial published in Nutrients by Di Pierro and colleagues: 30 adults with insomnia took a standardized lemon balm phytosome extract and a placebo for two weeks each, in random order, separated by a washout period, while a wearable device tracked their sleep. The active extract lowered Insomnia Severity Index scores by 2.9 points more than placebo, a statistically significant difference, and nearly nine in ten participants reported sleeping better, compared to three in ten on placebo. Slow-wave sleep — the deep, physically restorative stage — rose by roughly 15%, while REM sleep fell by about 10%. Anxiety and daytime activity levels didn't change significantly.

An earlier, smaller trial found a different population — patients recovering from coronary artery bypass surgery — reported less anxiety and better sleep quality after a week on a much higher dose (1,500 mg daily) of lemon balm versus placebo, though that short, specific setting doesn't necessarily generalize to ordinary insomnia. Combined with the newer trial's industry-linked authorship, the honest read is that lemon balm has a plausible mechanism and a genuinely promising small trial behind it, not yet the large, independently replicated evidence base of better-studied sleep herbs — making it a reasonable low-risk option to try, not a proven substitute for consistent sleep habits.

Further reading: Di Pierro et al., "Effects of Melissa officinalis Phytosome on Sleep Quality: Results of a Prospective, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled, and Cross-Over Study," Nutrients (2024) and Soltanpour et al., "Effects of Melissa Officinalis on Anxiety and Sleep Quality in Patients Undergoing Coronary Artery Bypass Surgery," Complementary Therapies in Clinical Practice (2019).
