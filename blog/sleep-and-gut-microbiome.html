Sleep and the Gut Microbiome: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2019 study in PLOS ONE led by researchers including Robert Smith found that greater overall diversity of gut bacteria was positively correlated with sleep efficiency and total sleep time, and negatively correlated with time spent awake after initially falling asleep.
  • A 2024 study published in the journal SLEEP found that night-to-night variability in sleep duration — not just short sleep on its own — was linked to lower microbial richness and diversity, suggesting an erratic schedule may matter as much as how long you sleep.
  • Researchers describe the relationship as a two-way street: certain gut bacteria produce compounds like serotonin, GABA and short-chain fatty acids that feed into the brain's sleep-regulating circuits, while poor sleep in turn appears to reshape the microbiome for the worse.

The idea that your gut and your sleep are connected sounds like a stretch until you look at what actually links them: the gut-brain axis, a constant back-and-forth of signals between the digestive tract and the central nervous system, carried partly by the trillions of bacteria living in the gut itself.

The clearest human evidence comes from a 2019 study published in PLOS ONE, which measured gut microbiome composition alongside objective sleep data from wearable trackers. The researchers found that people with more diverse gut bacteria tended to sleep more efficiently and for longer overall, while lower diversity tracked with more time spent lying awake after initially drifting off. The same study found several specific bacterial groups — including Lachnospiraceae, Corynebacterium and Blautia — that were negatively correlated with sleep quality measures, though the authors were careful to frame these as associations rather than proof that any one microbe causes poor sleep.

A more recent study in the journal SLEEP pushed the picture further by looking at consistency rather than just duration. It found that people whose sleep length varied more from night to night — and who spent more time awake during the night — tended to have less rich and less diverse gut microbiomes, even after accounting for total sleep time. That lines up with a broader pattern researchers have observed: an irregular sleep schedule appears to stress the gut environment in ways that a shorter but consistent one does not.

The mechanism researchers point to is metabolic. Certain gut bacteria, including strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, produce or support the production of serotonin, GABA and short-chain fatty acids — the same categories of compounds involved in regulating the brain's sleep-wake cycle. Other bacteria help metabolize tryptophan, the amino acid that's a precursor to both serotonin and melatonin. When the balance of these bacteria shifts, so does the supply of the raw materials the brain uses to regulate rest — and the relationship runs the other direction too, since disrupted sleep has been shown to alter gut bacterial composition within days. None of this points to a single supplement or fix; the research so far supports treating gut health and sleep as parts of the same system rather than two separate habits.

Further reading: Gut Microbiome Diversity Is Associated With Sleep Physiology in Humans (PLOS ONE) and Night-to-Night Sleep Duration Variability and Gut Microbial Diversity (SLEEP).
