Does Mattress Firmness Actually Change How You Sleep?

Quick takeaways
  • In a 313-person randomized trial published in The Lancet, adults with chronic low-back pain who slept on a medium-firm mattress had better odds of improved pain and disability at 90 days than those on a firm mattress.
  • A 2021 systematic review of 39 studies in the Journal of Orthopaedics and Traumatology found medium-firm mattresses were consistently linked to better comfort, sleep quality, and spinal alignment — but it also found no agreed-upon standard for exactly how firm "medium-firm" should be.
  • The Sleep Foundation notes firmness needs shift with body weight and sleep position, so the best mattress is the one that keeps your spine roughly neutral, not a single number on a showroom scale.

"Get a firm mattress for your back" is one of those pieces of advice that sounds like common sense and turns out to be backwards. The best-known test of it is a 2003 randomized, double-blind, controlled trial published in The Lancet: researchers assigned 313 adults with chronic, non-specific low-back pain to sleep on either a firm mattress or a medium-firm one for 90 days. The medium-firm group came out ahead, with roughly double the odds of reporting less pain in bed and less disability than the firm-mattress group. Pain on rising improved too, though that difference was smaller and not statistically significant on its own.

That single trial doesn't stand alone. A 2021 systematic review in the Journal of Orthopaedics and Traumatology pulled together 39 studies on mattresses, sleep quality, and spinal alignment and reached a similar conclusion: mattresses in the medium-firm range were the most consistent performers across comfort, reported sleep quality, and keeping the spine in a neutral position overnight. But the reviewers also flagged a real gap — there's no industry-wide standard for what "medium-firm" means in newtons or any other objective unit, so the same label on two mattresses can describe meaningfully different surfaces. "Medium-firm" is a useful range to aim for, not a precise spec you can shop by.

Firmness also isn't one-size-fits-all. The Sleep Foundation's guidance ties the right firmness to body weight and sleep position: heavier bodies and back or stomach sleepers generally need more support to avoid sinking into a swayed-back position, while lighter bodies and side sleepers often do better on something slightly softer that cushions the shoulder and hip instead of fighting them. The underlying goal in all of these studies is the same — a sleep surface that lets the spine stay close to its natural, neutral alignment all night, without pressure points forcing you to toss and turn to relieve them.

None of this means a specific mattress will fix insomnia or chronic pain on its own, and researchers studying this area consistently note that sample sizes are modest and self-reported pain is a noisy outcome to measure. But if a mattress is nearing the end of its usable life (the Sleep Foundation suggests most need replacing every six to eight years) and sleep or back pain has been getting worse, the evidence points in a specific direction: test something in the medium-firm range before assuming firmer is automatically better.

Further reading: Effect of firmness of mattress on chronic non-specific low-back pain (The Lancet, via PubMed) and What type of mattress should be chosen to avoid back pain and improve sleep quality? (Journal of Orthopaedics and Traumatology).
