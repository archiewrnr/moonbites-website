Sleepwalking: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis in PLOS ONE, pooling 51 studies and over 100,000 people, put lifetime sleepwalking prevalence at roughly 6.9% — and found it's still fairly common in adulthood, with about 1.5% reporting an episode in the past year, versus roughly 5% of children.
  • Sleepwalking happens during N3 sleep, the deepest slow-wave stage of the night, which is why episodes usually strike in the first third of the night and why sleepwalkers are so hard to rouse and rarely remember anything afterward.
  • Genetics appear to play a real role, and researchers consistently flag sleep deprivation, fever, stress, alcohol and certain medications as the most common triggers for an episode.

Sleepwalking, or somnambulism, covers a wider range of behavior than the cartoon image of someone walking with arms outstretched. Episodes can look like sitting up and mumbling, fumbling with objects, or walking through the house and even attempting simple tasks, all while the person stays largely unresponsive and typically has no memory of it the next morning. It belongs to a family of conditions called NREM parasomnias, alongside night terrors and confusional arousals, all of which surface out of deep sleep rather than dreams.

How common it is depends on age, but the numbers are higher than most people assume. The 2016 PLOS ONE meta-analysis, drawing on 51 studies and more than 100,000 participants, estimated lifetime prevalence at about 6.9%. Looking just at the past 12 months, roughly 5% of children had an episode compared with about 1.5% of adults — meaning sleepwalking doesn't disappear with childhood so much as become less frequent.

The mechanism comes down to timing. Sleepwalking emerges from N3, the deepest stage of non-REM sleep, which is concentrated in the first third of the night — this is why episodes cluster shortly after falling asleep rather than toward morning. During N3, parts of the brain responsible for movement and basic navigation can become active while the regions governing full awareness and memory formation stay offline, producing a partially awake, partially asleep state that explains both the blank stare and the total amnesia afterward.

Twin and family studies point to a meaningful genetic component, but genetics alone don't trigger an episode on a given night. The most consistently reported precipitants are sleep deprivation, fever, stress, alcohol, and certain medications, since anything that deepens or fragments slow-wave sleep raises the odds of an episode. Underlying sleep disorders that disrupt sleep architecture, including sleep apnea, have also been linked to higher rates of sleepwalking.

Mayo Clinic's practical guidance is simple: don't try to forcibly wake someone who is sleepwalking, since it isn't dangerous to wake them but can leave them confused or agitated — gently guide them back to bed instead. For households where sleepwalking is frequent, the bigger priority is safety-proofing: locking doors and windows, clearing tripping hazards, and considering a ground-floor bedroom. Frequent episodes, injury risk, or sleepwalking that starts for the first time in adulthood are all reasons to bring it up with a doctor.

Further reading: Prevalence of Sleepwalking: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (Stallman & Kohler, PLOS ONE) and Sleepwalking: Symptoms and Causes (Mayo Clinic).
