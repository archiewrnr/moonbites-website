Glycine and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2007 study in Sleep and Biological Rhythms found that 3 grams of glycine taken shortly before bed improved subjective sleep quality and shortened the time volunteers took to reach slow-wave sleep, without changing overall sleep architecture.
  • Follow-up research published in Neuropsychopharmacology identified a specific mechanism: glycine acts on NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain's master clock, triggering vasodilation that helps cool the body's core — mirroring the natural temperature drop that precedes sleep onset.
  • The clinical trial base is still small and short-term, and glycine isn't a substitute for treating a diagnosed sleep disorder — but it appears well tolerated and has a physiologically plausible mechanism behind it.

Glycine is the simplest and one of the most abundant amino acids in the body, occurring naturally in bone broth, gelatin, and other protein-rich foods. It's become a modest but recurring name in sleep research over the past two decades — not marketed as a sedative, but studied as something closer to a thermostat adjustment the body makes just before falling asleep.

The most-cited human trial, led by Yamadera and colleagues and published in Sleep and Biological Rhythms in 2007, gave adults with self-reported unsatisfactory sleep either 3 grams of glycine or a placebo about 30 minutes before bedtime. On the glycine nights, polysomnography recordings showed participants reached slow-wave sleep faster and reported feeling less fatigued and more clear-headed the next day, even though total time asleep barely changed. It's a small study — a common limitation across amino-acid sleep research — but its findings have held up as the reference point for most of the work that followed.

What explains the effect is more interesting than the effect itself. A 2015 study in Neuropsychopharmacology found glycine acts as a co-agonist at NMDA receptors inside the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain region that runs the body's circadian clock. Activating those receptors triggered vasodilation at the skin's surface in the study's animal models — warming the hands and feet while allowing core body temperature to drop. That core-temperature dip is one of the body's own signals that it's time to fall asleep, which is part of why a warm bath or warm feet before bed can help: both nudge the same cooling cascade.

None of this makes glycine a proven treatment. The trial base is still thin, mostly short in duration, and hasn't been tested against diagnosed insomnia the way structured therapies or medications have. What it does have going for it is a plausible, physiologically grounded mechanism and a good safety profile — an amino acid the body already makes and uses constantly, rather than a novel compound layered on top of normal biology.

Further reading: Glycine ingestion improves subjective sleep quality in human volunteers, correlating with polysomnographic changes (Sleep and Biological Rhythms) and The Sleep-Promoting and Hypothermic Effects of Glycine are Mediated by NMDA Receptors in the Suprachiasmatic Nucleus (Neuropsychopharmacology).
