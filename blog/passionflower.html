Passionflower and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2020 double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of 110 adults with diagnosed insomnia found that a passionflower extract significantly increased total sleep time measured by overnight polysomnography compared with placebo — one of the few herbal sleep aids with actual sleep-lab data behind it.
  • An earlier crossover study of 41 healthy adults found a week of nightly passionflower tea improved self-rated sleep quality over placebo tea, but the polysomnography readings taken from a subset of participants showed no measurable change — a reminder that "feels better" and "measures better" don't always move together.
  • The calming effect is thought to involve apigenin, a flavonoid in passionflower that interacts with GABA receptors in the brain, though NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes the overall evidence base is still limited and flags possible interactions with sedatives.

Passionflower doesn't get the attention chamomile or valerian root do, but it has something a lot of herbal sleep aids lack: at least one trial that measured sleep with electrodes instead of just a morning questionnaire. That doesn't make it a proven fix, but it does make the evidence worth a closer look.

The strongest data point is a 2020 study published in International Clinical Psychopharmacology. Researchers randomized 110 adults with diagnosed insomnia disorder to a passionflower extract or a placebo for two weeks, with everyone spending a night in a sleep lab hooked up to polysomnography before and after. Total sleep time increased significantly more in the passionflower group than the placebo group, and sleep efficiency and time spent awake after falling asleep both improved within the passionflower group over the two weeks, though those two measures weren't statistically better than placebo on their own.

That builds on an earlier, smaller study — a 2011 double-blind crossover trial in Phytotherapy Research in which 41 healthy young adults drank either passionflower tea or a placebo tea nightly for a week, then switched. Participants rated their sleep quality noticeably higher during the passionflower week. But when a subset of ten underwent overnight polysomnography on the final night of each treatment, the objective sleep measurements barely budged. It's a useful illustration of why researchers care about lab data at all: subjective sleep ratings and measured sleep don't always agree.

Mechanistically, passionflower is thought to act partly through apigenin, a flavonoid that binds weakly to GABA receptors — the same inhibitory system targeted by many prescription sleep and anxiety medications, though far less potently. NCCIH's official guidance is measured: it describes the evidence on passionflower for sleep and anxiety as limited, and cautions against combining it with other sedating substances, or using it before surgery or during pregnancy, since it may have added sedative or uterine effects. As with most botanical sleep aids, the honest summary is "modestly promising, not proven" — but passionflower is one of the few in that category with real sleep-lab numbers to point to.

Further reading: Passionflower: Usefulness and Safety (NIH NCCIH) and Effects of Passiflora incarnata Linnaeus on Polysomnographic Sleep Parameters in Subjects With Insomnia Disorder (International Clinical Psychopharmacology).
