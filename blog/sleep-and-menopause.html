Sleep and Menopause: What the Research Says About Hot Flashes and Insomnia

Quick takeaways
  • A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Neurology found that roughly 72% of perimenopausal women report a sleep disorder, with close to 40% reporting poor overall sleep quality.
  • The same review found hot flashes were identified as a contributing cause of insomnia in about 80% of the perimenopausal women who experienced them — not just an annoyance that happens alongside poor sleep, but a driver of it.
  • A 2021 study in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine (the EPISONO study) found hot flashes were roughly twice as common among women with a diagnosed insomnia disorder (25.5%) as among good sleepers (12.6%), reinforcing the link with objective diagnostic data rather than self-report alone.

Sleep is often expected to just get harder as people get older, but the disruption many women experience during the menopausal transition isn't ordinary aging — it's a specific, hormonally-driven pattern with its own mechanism, and its own timeline.

Estrogen and progesterone don't just govern reproduction; both interact with the brain systems that regulate sleep and body temperature. Progesterone has a mild sedative effect, partly through its influence on GABA activity, and its decline during perimenopause removes some of that natural calming support. Estrogen, meanwhile, helps stabilize the hypothalamus's temperature set point. As estrogen fluctuates and eventually drops, that set point becomes less stable, and the brain starts triggering hot flashes — sudden dilation of blood vessels and sweating — in response to temperature swings that wouldn't have registered before. A hot flash in the middle of the night is, physiologically, a thermoregulatory event that happens to wake you up.

That's borne out in the research at scale. The 2025 Frontiers in Neurology review found sleep disorders in nearly three-quarters of perimenopausal women, and traced a large share of that back to hot flashes specifically. The EPISONO study adds a useful detail: hot flashes weren't just more common among women who felt they slept badly, they were roughly twice as common among women who met the clinical criteria for insomnia disorder. The perimenopausal transition also carries a higher risk of other sleep conditions, including obstructive sleep apnea and restless legs syndrome, according to the Sleep Foundation — so persistent sleep trouble during this window isn't automatically "just hot flashes" and is worth mentioning to a clinician.

None of this means disrupted sleep during perimenopause has to be endured indefinitely. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has evidence specifically in menopausal populations, including for insomnia tied to nighttime hot flashes, and hormone therapy — discussed with a doctor, weighing individual risks and benefits — remains the most effective treatment for hot flashes themselves. A cooler bedroom and moisture-wicking bedding won't fix the underlying hormonal shift, but they work directly on the thermoregulatory piece of the problem, which is often the piece keeping people awake.

Further reading: Factors Influencing Sleep Disorders in Perimenopausal Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (Frontiers in Neurology) and Hot Flashes, Insomnia, and the Reproductive Stages: The EPISONO Study (Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine).
