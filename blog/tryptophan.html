Tryptophan and Sleep: What the Turkey Myth Gets Wrong

Quick takeaways
  • Tryptophan is an essential amino acid your body converts into serotonin, which is then converted into melatonin — but only a small fraction of what you eat is actually routed down that pathway.
  • A 2022 meta-analysis in Nutrition Reviews found tryptophan supplementation, especially at doses of 1 gram or more per day, was linked to shorter time spent awake after sleep onset and modest gains in sleep quality.
  • Turkey isn't unusually high in tryptophan — pork, cheese, and eggs contain as much or more per serving — so a large, carb-heavy meal (and any alcohol with it) is a better explanation for post-holiday drowsiness than the turkey itself.

Every November, someone explains that turkey makes you sleepy because of its tryptophan content. The amino acid part is real — tryptophan genuinely sits upstream of sleep chemistry. The turkey part is where the story falls apart.

Tryptophan is an essential amino acid, meaning your body can't make it and has to get it from food: poultry, eggs, cheese, fish, nuts, seeds, and soy all contain it. Once absorbed, a portion of that tryptophan crosses into the brain, where it's converted first into serotonin and then, later, into melatonin. That's the real mechanistic link between tryptophan and sleep. But tryptophan also competes with other amino acids for the same transporter into the brain, and under ordinary eating conditions, only a modest share of what you consume ends up going down the serotonin-melatonin route rather than being used elsewhere in the body. A single meal's worth rarely moves the needle much.

The clinical picture is more interesting than the folklore. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis published in Nutrition Reviews pooled data from controlled trials and found that tryptophan supplementation was associated with a meaningful reduction in wake-after-sleep-onset — the time spent lying awake once you've already fallen asleep — with the clearest effects showing up at doses of roughly 1 gram per day or more. That's substantially more concentrated tryptophan than you'd get from a dinner plate; it's closer to what a measured supplement delivers than what a serving of turkey or chicken provides.

Which is also why the turkey myth doesn't hold up on its own terms. Ounce for ounce, turkey isn't even the richest source — pork, beef, and cheese contain comparable or higher amounts. What actually tends to follow a big holiday meal is a large volume of food, a heavy load of refined carbohydrates, and often alcohol, all of which independently promote drowsiness. Some researchers note that carbohydrates can indirectly help tryptophan's case by triggering an insulin response that clears competing amino acids from the bloodstream, giving tryptophan an easier path across the blood-brain barrier — so the stuffing and mashed potatoes may be doing more supporting work than the turkey gets credit for.

Further reading: The Impact of Tryptophan Supplementation on Sleep Quality: A Systematic Review, Meta-Analysis and Meta-Regression (Nutrition Reviews) and What Is Tryptophan? (Sleep Foundation).
