Sleep and Creativity: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2004 Nature study by Wagner and colleagues found that more than twice as many participants discovered a hidden shortcut rule in a number task after a period of sleep compared with an equivalent stretch of wakefulness, whether the break happened at night or during the day.
  • A 2009 PNAS study by Cai, Mednick and colleagues found that only naps containing REM sleep improved performance on the Remote Associates Test, a standard measure of associative thinking, by roughly 40% — naps without REM and quiet rest produced no improvement at all.
  • The effect isn't universal: a 2020 Frontiers in Psychology study found no sleep advantage for open-ended, loosely defined problems, suggesting sleep may specifically help with problems that have a discoverable hidden solution rather than creativity in general.

"Sleep on it" is one of the oldest pieces of advice for a stuck problem, and it holds up better under laboratory testing than most folk wisdom about the brain. Over the past two decades, sleep researchers have designed tasks specifically to test whether sleep changes problem-solving, rather than relying on anecdotes about ideas arriving fully formed after a good night's rest.

The clearest early evidence comes from a 2004 study by Ullrich Wagner and colleagues, published in Nature. Participants trained on a number-reduction task that could be solved slowly through repeated calculation, or almost instantly once someone spotted a hidden shortcut rule built into its structure. After a break of either nighttime sleep, nighttime wakefulness, or daytime wakefulness, participants were retested on the same task. More than twice as many people who had slept discovered the shortcut compared with those who stayed awake, regardless of what time of day the break occurred — pointing to sleep itself, rather than simply time passing, as the relevant factor.

A 2009 study in PNAS by Denise Cai, Sara Mednick and colleagues narrowed the question to which stage of sleep matters. Using the Remote Associates Test, which measures the kind of flexible, associative thinking involved in noticing unexpected connections between words, participants napped with REM sleep, napped without reaching REM, or simply rested quietly before being retested in the afternoon. Only the group that reached REM sleep showed a meaningful gain, improving by about 40% over their morning scores, while the no-REM-nap and quiet-rest groups showed none — suggesting REM specifically, not sleep or rest in general, was doing the work.

None of this means sleep boosts every kind of creativity. A 2020 study in Frontiers in Psychology found no sleep advantage for open-ended, ill-defined problems that lack a single hidden answer, which suggests the benefit is strongest for problems with a discoverable solution rather than creative thinking as a whole. Researchers' leading explanation is that REM sleep loosens and reshuffles associative networks in memory, making distant or unlikely connections easier to notice — a plausible mechanism, but one still short of being fully proven.

Further reading: Wagner et al., "Sleep inspires insight," Nature (2004) and Cai et al., "REM, not incubation, improves creativity by priming associative networks," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (2009).
