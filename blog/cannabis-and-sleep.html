Cannabis and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews, pooling nine studies, found cannabis use did not consistently change sleep duration, latency, time awake, efficiency, or how sleep stages were distributed.
  • The long-held idea that THC reliably suppresses REM sleep came mostly from small, older trials using high doses — the 2025 review notes those studies had real methodological limits.
  • The most consistent finding, across both the 2025 review and an earlier 2014 systematic review, sits on the other side of use: stopping regular cannabis reliably disrupts sleep, with shorter total sleep time, longer time to fall asleep, and a rebound in REM.

Cannabis is one of the most commonly used sleep aids that isn't actually marketed as one — people reach for it informally, often based on the assumption that it reliably knocks them out faster but costs them some REM sleep in the process. That assumption has circulated for decades, but a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in Sleep Medicine Reviews set out to check how well it actually holds up against the polysomnography data — the lab-measured sleep recordings researchers use to track real sleep stages, not just self-reports.

The reviewers, led by Rob Velzeboer, identified 18 studies and found nine suitable for a formal meta-analysis. Their main conclusion was that cannabis administration did not consistently change total sleep duration, sleep latency, time spent awake, sleep efficiency, or the proportion of time spent in each sleep stage. The earlier reports of reduced REM sleep that shaped the conventional wisdom came mainly from small trials that used high THC doses, and the review flagged real methodological limits in that older body of work — a reason to be cautious about treating "cannabis suppresses REM" as settled fact.

That uncertainty isn't new. A 2014 systematic review in the same journal, led by Gates, Albertella and Copeland, had already looked at 39 publications on cannabinoids and sleep and concluded the evidence was inconclusive, with inconsistent results and study designs too weak to support firm conclusions. More than a decade and a larger, more rigorous review later, the honest answer is still that cannabis's effect on sleep architecture during use isn't well pinned down.

What both reviews agree on is what happens when regular use stops. Withdrawal from habitual cannabis use was consistently linked to disrupted sleep — less total sleep, longer time to fall asleep, and a rebound in REM sleep as the body adjusts. For anyone using cannabis nightly and sleeping reasonably well, that's worth knowing before attributing any later sleep trouble to something else: cutting back may be the more likely explanation than it first seems.

Further reading: Cannabis and sleep architecture: A systematic review and meta-analysis (Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2025) and The effects of cannabinoid administration on sleep: A systematic review of human studies (Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2014).
