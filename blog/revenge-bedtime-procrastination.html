Revenge Bedtime Procrastination: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • "Bedtime procrastination" was named in a 2014 study of 177 adults, which defined it as delaying sleep with no external reason for doing so — and linked it to weaker self-regulation and more next-day fatigue.
  • A more recent 14-day diary study of 280 undergraduates, published in the journal SLEEP, found that delaying bedtime roughly doubled the odds of daytime sleepiness the next day and was tied to worse mood.
  • That same research pushes back on the idea that it's pure willpower failure — much of it looks like a deliberate trade, reclaiming personal time after a day that didn't feel like your own, rather than mindless scrolling.

The urge to stay up late even when you're tired and have nowhere to be in the morning has a name in sleep research: bedtime procrastination. Dutch researcher Floor Kroese and colleagues introduced the term in a 2014 paper in Frontiers in Psychology, defining it as going to bed later than intended without any external reason forcing the delay — as opposed to insomnia, where someone wants to sleep but can't. In a community sample of 177 adults, the tendency was linked to lower self-regulation and predicted more reports of insufficient sleep and daytime fatigue, independent of how much people generally struggled with self-control elsewhere in life.

A newer study, published in the journal SLEEP, followed 280 undergraduates for two weeks using nightly sleep diaries. On nights participants delayed their bedtime, they had roughly double the odds of reporting daytime sleepiness the following day, along with more negative mood and less positive mood. But the researchers also complicated the "just use more willpower" story: when they asked people why they'd stayed up, the reasons split into a few distinct patterns — some mindless (losing track of time on a screen), some strategic (finishing a task before a hard deadline), and some deliberate, where people knowingly traded sleep for time that felt like their own.

That last pattern is what's picked up the nickname "revenge bedtime procrastination" — staying up late specifically to reclaim personal time after a day spent on other people's schedules, even while fully aware it will cost sleep. It tends to show up most in people who feel they have little control over their daytime hours, which is part of why simply telling someone to "just go to bed earlier" rarely works; the behavior isn't really about the bed.

Because it isn't a falling-asleep problem, the fixes look different from standard sleep hygiene advice. The more useful lever, per this research, is protecting some real downtime earlier in the day so night doesn't become the only window for it, and picking a concrete "screens off" cue rather than a vague intended bedtime.

Further reading: Why We Postpone Sleep: Bedtime Procrastination Beyond Self-Control Failure (SLEEP, via PMC) and Revenge Bedtime Procrastination: Why You Self-Sabotage at Night (Sleep Foundation).
