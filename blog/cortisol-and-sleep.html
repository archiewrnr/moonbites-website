Cortisol and Sleep: How Your Stress Hormone Rhythm Shapes Rest

Quick takeaways
  • Cortisol isn't just a "stress hormone" — it runs on a 24-hour rhythm, peaking in the 30-60 minutes after waking (called the cortisol awakening response, or CAR) and gradually falling to its lowest point during nighttime sleep.
  • The relationship with sleep runs in both directions: elevated evening cortisol from unresolved stress can delay sleep onset, and short or poor-quality sleep is linked to a blunted morning cortisol rise the next day.
  • A 2025 systematic review pooling studies on sleep and cortisol regulation found that people with lower sleep quality consistently showed a smaller morning cortisol rise and a flatter overall daily curve than well-rested people.

Cortisol gets talked about almost entirely as "the stress hormone," which undersells what it actually does. It's also one of the body's main timing signals — a hormone that's supposed to rise and fall on a predictable schedule every 24 hours, working alongside your circadian clock to tell your body when to be alert and when to power down.

In a healthy rhythm, cortisol is lowest during the deepest part of nighttime sleep, then climbs sharply in the 30 to 60 minutes after you wake up — a well-studied spike researchers call the cortisol awakening response. From there it tapers gradually across the day, reaching its low point again by the time you're asleep. That evening low is part of what's supposed to make falling asleep possible: cortisol is an alerting hormone, so when it stays elevated at the hour it should be dropping, it works against the processes that bring on sleep.

This is where chronic stress complicates things. When stress keeps cortisol elevated into the evening — instead of following its normal downward slope — it can make it measurably harder to wind down, independent of how physically tired you feel. But the relationship doesn't just run one way. A 2025 systematic review in the journal Healthcare, which pooled data from multiple studies on sleep and cortisol regulation, found that people with lower sleep quality consistently showed a flatter cortisol awakening response and a smaller decline in cortisol across the day than people with higher sleep quality — meaning a bad night doesn't just cost you rest, it can blunt the next morning's hormonal "start the day" signal and flatten the rhythm that's supposed to reset by evening. Left unaddressed, that pattern can become self-reinforcing: poor sleep flattens the cortisol curve, and a flattened curve makes good sleep harder to get back.

None of this means the occasional stressful day will wreck your sleep long-term — the cortisol rhythm is resilient and resets with consistent, good-quality sleep. But it's a reminder that what's happening with stress during the day doesn't stay contained to the day. The wind-down window before bed isn't just a scheduling habit; it's giving cortisol time to actually finish its normal descent.

Further reading: A Systematic Review of Literature on the Association Among Sleep, Cortisol Level and Cardiovascular Health Within the Healthcare Shift Worker Population (Healthcare, via PMC) and The Functional and Clinical Significance of the 24-Hour Rhythm of Circulating Glucocorticoids (PMC).
