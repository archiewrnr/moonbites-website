Segmented Sleep: Why Waking Up at 3 A.M. Might Be Older Than the Light Bulb

Quick takeaways
  • Historian A. Roger Ekirch spent over a decade combing diaries, court depositions, medical texts, and literature from the 15th through 18th centuries, and documented thousands of references to a "first sleep" and "second sleep" — two nightly bouts separated by roughly an hour of quiet wakefulness.
  • In a 1992 study at the National Institute of Mental Health, psychiatrist Thomas Wehr kept healthy adults on an artificially long night — 14 hours of darkness instead of the usual 8 — for several weeks. Their sleep spontaneously split into two segments with 1 to 3 hours of calm waking in between, and the biological night (as marked by melatonin release) lengthened to match.
  • Both lines of evidence point the same way: a single unbroken 8-hour block may be a habit shaped by artificial light and modern schedules, not a fixed biological requirement — so a brief, calm waking in the middle of the night isn't automatically a sign that something is wrong.

Waking at 2 or 3 a.m. and staying awake for a stretch is one of the most common complaints people bring to sleep clinics, usually framed as a symptom to fix. Two separate bodies of research — one historical, one experimental — suggest it may instead be closer to a default setting than a malfunction.

The historical case comes from Roger Ekirch, a historian at Virginia Tech who spent years searching pre-industrial records for how people actually described their nights. What he found was strikingly consistent: households across Europe would retire not long after dark, sleep for a few hours in a "first sleep," wake for an hour or so in the middle of the night, and then settle into a "second sleep" until dawn. The waking interval wasn't treated as a problem — it was ordinary time for prayer, quiet conversation, tending a fire, or simply lying in the dark before drifting off again. References to it largely disappear from Western sources by the mid-1800s, roughly tracking the spread of artificial lighting and factory schedules.

The experimental support came later and independently. Thomas Wehr, at NIMH, wanted to see what would happen to sleep if modern light exposure were removed rather than just theorized about. He put study participants through several weeks of 14-hour nights with no artificial light — closer to what a pre-industrial winter night actually looked like. Rather than sleeping through the extra darkness in one long stretch, participants' sleep reorganized itself into two distinct bouts with a period of quiet, melatonin-driven wakefulness between them, echoing Ekirch's historical accounts almost exactly.

None of this is an argument to abandon consolidated sleep — most modern schedules, jobs, and school start times simply don't accommodate a multi-hour gap in the middle of the night, and consistently timed sleep still matters. But for anyone who wakes briefly at 3 a.m. and immediately starts worrying about it, the research offers a genuinely different frame: a short, calm waking in the night has deep precedent, and worrying about it may do more to keep you up than the waking itself.

Further reading: Wehr, "In short photoperiods, human sleep is biphasic," Journal of Sleep Research (1992), via PubMed and A. Roger Ekirch's research summary on segmented sleep (Virginia Tech).
