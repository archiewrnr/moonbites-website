Vitamin D and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis in Nutrients, pooling multiple observational studies, found people with vitamin D deficiency had a significantly higher risk of sleep disorders overall (OR 1.50), including poor sleep quality (OR 1.59), short sleep duration (OR 1.74), and excessive daytime sleepiness (OR 1.36).
  • A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, also in Nutrients, found vitamin D supplementation produced a statistically significant improvement in Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores versus placebo (mean difference −2.33), with the authors rating the evidence moderate-certainty.
  • Researchers point to vitamin D receptors in brain regions involved in sleep-wake regulation and a possible role in melatonin synthesis as candidate mechanisms — but most experts stress supplementation appears to help mainly in people who are actually deficient, not as a general-purpose sleep aid.

Vitamin D is best known for bone health and calcium absorption, made in the skin from sun exposure or obtained from food and supplements. Over the past decade, a steady stream of research has pointed to a second role: receptors for vitamin D turn up throughout the brain, including in regions tied to the sleep-wake cycle, and low vitamin D status keeps showing up alongside worse sleep in population studies.

The strongest evidence for the deficiency side comes from a 2018 meta-analysis published in Nutrients, which pooled data across multiple observational studies. People classified as vitamin D deficient had a 50% higher relative risk of having a sleep disorder of some kind compared with those with adequate levels, with the association holding up separately for poor sleep quality, short sleep duration, and daytime sleepiness. Because these are observational studies, they show an association rather than proof that low vitamin D directly causes worse sleep — people who are deficient may also spend less time outdoors, have other health conditions, or share other factors that independently affect sleep.

That's where intervention trials matter more. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis, also in Nutrients, focused specifically on randomized controlled trials giving people vitamin D supplements versus placebo. It found a statistically significant improvement in sleep quality scores on the well-validated Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, with moderate-certainty evidence — a meaningfully stronger standard than the observational data alone. That said, the same body of trial evidence was less consistent on other outcomes like sleep-related impairment and restless legs syndrome, and the researchers were careful to note more work is needed before vitamin D can be called a proven sleep treatment.

The practical takeaway from both bodies of evidence points the same direction: vitamin D deficiency is common, especially in winter months or for people who spend most of their time indoors, and correcting a genuine deficiency looks like it can modestly improve sleep quality. But there's little evidence that piling on extra vitamin D helps if your levels are already normal, and very high doses can cause toxicity. A blood test is the only reliable way to know where you actually stand.

Further reading: The Association between Vitamin D Deficiency and Sleep Disorders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (Nutrients) and Vitamin D Supplementation and Sleep: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Intervention Studies (Nutrients).
