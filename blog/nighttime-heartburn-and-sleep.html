Nighttime Heartburn and Sleep: Why Reflux Gets Worse After Dark

Quick takeaways
  • Lying flat removes gravity's help keeping stomach acid down, and saliva production and swallowing — both part of how the body normally clears acid from the esophagus — slow down during sleep.
  • In a survey cited by the Sleep Foundation, nearly 60% of people with frequent heartburn said it disrupted their sleep, and more than 30% said it hurt their ability to function the next day.
  • Sleeping on your left side and avoiding lying down for about three hours after eating have the most consistent support; a 2021 systematic review found evidence for head-of-bed elevation specifically was promising but limited by small, lower-quality trials.

Heartburn is common enough that it's easy to treat as background noise, but the version that shows up after you lie down is a distinct problem with its own mechanics — and its own fixes. Mayo Clinic notes that reflux symptoms often get worse at night or while lying down, and persistent nighttime reflux can bring along an ongoing cough, laryngitis, or worsening asthma, on top of disrupted sleep itself.

The reason it's worse after dark comes down to physics and physiology working against you at the same time. Upright, gravity helps keep stomach contents where they belong. Lying flat removes that help, and the Sleep Foundation notes that saliva production drops and swallowing becomes less frequent during deeper sleep — removing a second mechanism the body normally uses to wash acid back down. Research on sleep position adds a wrinkle: lying on the right side is thought to make reflux more likely, because it can position stomach contents closer to the junction with the esophagus, while the left side is considered the more favorable position for keeping that junction above the acid pool.

On the evidence for what actually helps, Mayo Clinic's self-care guidance is specific: start the night on your left side, and avoid lying down for roughly three hours after eating. Head-of-bed elevation — raising the entire top of the bed a few inches, not just stacking pillows — is a longer-standing recommendation, but a 2021 systematic review in BMC Family Practice found that the available trials were small and at meaningful risk of bias, so while the authors still called it a cheap, low-risk option worth trying, they stopped short of a strong recommendation. Separately, research has turned up a link between GERD and obstructive sleep apnea, though it's still unclear whether one causes the other or they simply share risk factors.

None of this requires overhauling your evening — the changes with the best support are the simplest ones. If heartburn is interrupting sleep more than occasionally, or you're reaching for antacids more than twice a week, that's also the Mayo Clinic's threshold for talking to a doctor rather than just adjusting your sleep position.

Further reading: Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD): Diagnosis and Treatment (Mayo Clinic) and GERD and Sleep (Sleep Foundation).
