Doomscrolling and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2025 study in BMC Psychology found doomscrolling was linked to poorer sleep quality, and that "nomophobia" — anxiety about being without your phone — was a significant predictor of that link, while fear of missing out (FOMO) was not.
  • A randomized pilot trial found that college students who avoided their phones for just 30 minutes before bed for four weeks fell asleep faster, slept longer, reported better sleep quality, and showed lower pre-sleep mental arousal than a control group.
  • The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2025 survey found a sizable share of U.S. adults say reading news on a phone or tablet before bed makes their sleep worse — most of all among the youngest adults surveyed.

Doomscrolling — the habit of compulsively consuming a stream of mostly negative news or social content — gets blamed on screens in general, but the mechanism researchers are now zeroing in on isn't really about light. It's about what's on the screen. Scrolling through alarming or emotionally charged content before bed appears to work against sleep less by suppressing melatonin and more by keeping the mind switched on, which is a separate problem from the one blue-light-blocking glasses are built to solve.

A 2025 study published in BMC Psychology examined the relationship between doomscrolling, FOMO, nomophobia, and sleep quality. It found doomscrolling was positively associated with poorer sleep, and that nomophobia — the discomfort or anxiety of being separated from your phone — came out as a significant independent predictor of that relationship. Fear of missing out, often assumed to be the main culprit, did not reach significance in the same model. That's a useful distinction: the drive to keep scrolling may have less to do with not wanting to miss something and more to do with not wanting to be without the device at all.

There's also experimental evidence that cutting the habit helps. A randomized pilot trial, published in PLOS ONE, recruited college students with poor sleep who regularly used their phones at bedtime and split them into two groups: one instructed to avoid phone use in the 30 minutes before bed for four weeks, and a control group given no such instruction. The intervention group saw improvements across the board — shorter time to fall asleep, longer sleep duration, better self-reported sleep quality, lower pre-sleep arousal, and better next-day working memory. The sample was small, as is typical for a pilot study, but the direction of every outcome pointed the same way.

Survey data backs up the subjective side of this. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2025 sleep prioritization survey found that a notable share of U.S. adults say reading news on their phone or tablet before bed actively makes their sleep worse, with adults in their twenties the most likely to say so. The group's practical advice is unglamorous but consistent with the research: keep screens, especially ones loaded with newsfeeds, out of the last half hour or so before lights out, and consider charging the phone outside the bedroom entirely.

Further reading: The role of FoMO and nomophobia in explaining the relationship between doomscrolling and poor sleep quality (BMC Psychology, 2025) and Effect of restricting bedtime mobile phone use on sleep, arousal, mood, and working memory: A randomized pilot trial (PLOS ONE, 2020).
