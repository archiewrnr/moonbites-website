Sleep and Vaccine Response: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2023 meta-analysis in Current Biology pooling seven earlier studies found that sleeping fewer than six hours a night around the time of vaccination was linked to a measurably weaker antibody response than sleeping seven or more — an effect that held clearest when sleep was measured objectively rather than self-reported, and more pronounced in men than women.
  • In a 2003 hepatitis A vaccine trial, participants who slept normally the night after their shot had nearly double the antibody titer four weeks later compared with a group who stayed awake that single night — showing the timing matters after vaccination, not just before it.
  • Researchers trace part of the mechanism to hormones released during sleep, including growth hormone and prolactin, which appear to support immune cell activity in the lymphoid tissue where antibody production actually happens.

Vaccines work by giving the immune system a harmless preview of a pathogen and waiting for it to build a memory response it can call on later. It turns out how well you slept in the days around that shot has a measurable effect on how well that memory takes hold — not a fringe theory, but something a handful of controlled trials and prospective studies have tracked directly, with a 2023 pooled analysis finally putting numbers behind what researchers had suspected for two decades.

That analysis, published in Current Biology, combined seven earlier studies — four in which participants' sleep was directly manipulated in a lab, three that tracked people's ordinary sleep habits — covering influenza and hepatitis vaccinations in adults aged 18 to 60. The pattern held across both types of study: sleeping under six hours a night around vaccination was associated with a meaningfully weaker antibody response than sleeping seven hours or more, with the effect clearest when sleep was measured objectively rather than by questionnaire.

One of the studies behind that pooled result showed how directly the timing matters. Nineteen participants received a hepatitis A vaccine; half then slept normally that night, half stayed awake until 9pm the following day. Four weeks later, the group that slept had almost double the antibody titer of the group that didn't — the result of one missed night. Researchers linked part of the effect to hormones released during sleep, including growth hormone and prolactin, which appear to support the proliferation of immune cells in the lymphoid tissue where antibody production takes place.

None of this is a reason to delay a vaccine over a bad night's sleep — the effect sizes involved are meaningful but not dramatic, and researchers frame it as a low-cost way to get more out of a shot you're already getting, not a precondition for it. The practical takeaway looks a lot like how people already treat the days before a big exam: worth protecting a normal sleep schedule in the days immediately before and after vaccination, rather than treating it as incidental.

Further reading: A meta-analysis of the associations between insufficient sleep duration and antibody response to vaccination (Current Biology) and Sleep & Immunity: Can a Lack of Sleep Make You Sick? (Sleep Foundation).
