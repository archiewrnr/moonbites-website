Valerian Root and Sleep: What the Evidence Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Valerian is thought to act partly through the GABA system, similar in principle to how some anti-anxiety medications work, though researchers say the exact mechanism isn't fully worked out.
  • A 2020 systematic review pooling 60 studies and roughly 6,900 participants found valerian could modestly improve subjective sleep quality and anxiety, with no severe adverse events reported.
  • Despite that, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2017 clinical guideline recommends against using valerian for chronic insomnia, and NIH's own review calls the overall evidence inconsistent.

Valerian root has been used as a sleep aid for centuries, and unlike many traditional remedies, it's actually been studied extensively — dozens of clinical trials, spanning decades. What makes it an interesting case isn't a lack of research; it's that the research points in two directions at once, and the honest answer to "does it work" depends on exactly what you're asking it to do.

The proposed mechanism centers on gamma-aminobutyric acid, or GABA, the brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter. Compounds in valerian are believed to interact with GABA-A receptors — the same receptor family targeted by benzodiazepine medications, though far more mildly — which is the leading explanation for valerian's calming effect. NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes that the precise mechanism remains unclear, since valerian root contains many different compounds and isolating which ones drive the effect has proven difficult.

On the positive side, a 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis published in Sleep Science and Practice pooled 60 studies covering roughly 6,900 participants and found valerian could meaningfully improve subjective sleep quality and reduce anxiety, with no severe adverse events reported across the pooled data. The same review noted that results across individual trials were inconsistent, and suggested this variability may partly come down to the quality and type of valerian extract used — whole root or rhizome preparations appeared to produce more reliable effects than some processed extracts.

Yet when the American Academy of Sleep Medicine issued its 2017 clinical practice guideline on treating chronic insomnia, it specifically recommended against using valerian, citing insufficient evidence of benefit weighed against the available data. NIH's NCCIH similarly concludes that the evidence for valerian is inconsistent and that there isn't enough data to confirm it's useful for any specific health condition. The two conclusions aren't actually contradictory: general sleep-quality improvement across a mixed population is a different, lower bar than proving an effect on diagnosed chronic insomnia specifically, and valerian's trial base is stronger on the former than the latter. Valerian appears to be well tolerated overall, but the honest summary is that it's a reasonable, low-risk option with modest supporting evidence — not a proven treatment for a clinical sleep disorder.

Further reading: Valerian: Usefulness and Safety (NIH NCCIH) and Valerian Root in Treating Sleep Problems and Associated Disorders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (Sleep Science and Practice).
