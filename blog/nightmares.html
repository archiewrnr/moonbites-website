Nightmares: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Nightmare disorder — frequent, vivid, distressing dreams that wake you up — is different from an occasional bad dream. A 2020 review in Neurotherapeutics puts its prevalence at roughly 5% of the general population, rising substantially in people with PTSD and other psychiatric conditions.
  • Researchers haven't settled on one confirmed cause. Nightmares occur during REM sleep and are linked to stress, anxiety, trauma, sleep deprivation, and a family history of nightmares or other parasomnias.
  • The best-supported non-drug treatment is imagery rehearsal therapy. A randomized trial of 70 adults found a telephone-guided version improved nightmare frequency, nightmare-related distress, and even general insomnia severity compared with a wait-list.

Nightmares happen during REM sleep, usually in the second half of the night, which is part of why you tend to wake up and remember them vividly — unlike night terrors, a separate non-REM parasomnia that produces thrashing or screaming with little memory afterward. Most adults have an occasional nightmare without it meaning much of anything. Population surveys suggest only a small share, somewhere around 2 to 6%, have them on a weekly basis, even though a much larger group — something like a third to nearly half of adults — report at least one in a given month.

When nightmares become frequent, distressing, and disruptive enough to interfere with sleep or daytime functioning, clinicians diagnose nightmare disorder, a recognized parasomnia in its own right. A 2020 review in Neurotherapeutics estimates its prevalence at around 5% in the general population, but that figure climbs substantially among people with PTSD and other psychiatric conditions, where nightmares tend to be both more common and more closely tied to the underlying condition rather than a separate, isolated problem.

Despite how common nightmares are, researchers don't point to one confirmed cause. What's clearer is the pattern around them: nightmares cluster around stress, anxiety, and trauma, and sleep deprivation itself can make them more likely, partly through "REM rebound" — when sleep catches up after a period of lost sleep, or after stopping a medication that had been suppressing REM, the brain can spend unusually long stretches in REM, producing more vivid and often more unpleasant dream content. A family history of nightmares or other parasomnias also raises the odds, pointing to some heritable component on top of situational triggers.

An occasional nightmare usually doesn't need treatment of its own — it's typically a byproduct of a stressful stretch that passes with it. For nightmare disorder, the best-supported non-drug approach is imagery rehearsal therapy: while awake, a person deliberately rewrites the ending of a recurring nightmare into something less distressing and mentally rehearses that new version repeatedly, with the goal of eventually displacing the original script. A randomized trial of 70 adults with frequent nightmares tested a scaled-down version of this — three sessions delivered over five weeks with brief telephone check-ins — against a wait-list, and found the treatment group improved more on nightmare frequency, nightmare-related distress, and general insomnia severity, suggesting the benefit isn't limited to the dream content alone.

None of this means every bad dream calls for intervention. But if nightmares are frequent enough to cost you sleep or make you dread going to bed, the evidence points to a treatable pattern — not something to just wait out.

Further reading: Nightmare Disorder and Isolated Sleep Paralysis (Neurotherapeutics, via PMC) and Telephone-guided imagery rehearsal therapy for nightmares: Efficacy and mediator of change (Journal of Sleep Research, via PMC).
