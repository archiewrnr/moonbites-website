Orthosomnia: When Tracking Your Sleep Starts Hurting It

Quick takeaways
  • Sleep researchers coined "orthosomnia" in a 2017 case series after seeing patients fixate on tracker-reported sleep scores — including one woman whose device and own perception both called her night "poor," while a lab sleep study the same night showed she'd gotten substantial deep sleep.
  • A 2025 American Academy of Sleep Medicine survey found 48% of U.S. adults now use a sleep tracker (up from 35% in 2023), 55% change their behavior based on what it shows, and 76% say they've lost sleep worrying about their sleep.
  • Orthosomnia isn't a formal medical diagnosis, and a cross-sectional study of 523 people found how common it looks depends heavily on the criteria used — anywhere from about 3% to 14% of the sample.

Wearable and app-based sleep trackers have gone from a niche gadget to something close to a default: the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2025 survey put U.S. adult usage at 48%, up sharply from 35% just two years earlier. More than half of those users say the numbers change what they actually do at night — going to bed earlier, canceling plans, or lying still longer to try to raise a score.

That shift in behavior is exactly what caught the attention of sleep physician Kelly Baron and colleagues at Rush University, who described the pattern in a 2017 case series in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. They named it "orthosomnia," borrowing from orthorexia — the unhealthy fixation on eating perfectly — to describe patients who showed up convinced something was wrong with their sleep because a device told them so, even when it wasn't. In one case, a 27-year-old patient felt chronically unrefreshed after nights her tracker scored poorly; a night in a sleep lab told a different story, showing she was actually getting a healthy amount of deep sleep. The device, and her trust in it, had become a less reliable narrator than her own body.

Part of the problem is technical. Consumer trackers mostly infer sleep from movement and heart rate, not brainwaves, so they're prone to counting quiet wakefulness — lying still, reading, scrolling — as sleep, and can struggle to tell light sleep from true wakefulness. Part of it is behavioral: chasing a better score can mean spending more time in bed than the body actually needs, which research on insomnia treatment has long linked to worse, not better, sleep. The AASM's 2025 data captures the downstream effect bluntly: 76% of adults said they'd lost sleep specifically worrying about their sleep.

None of this means tracking data is useless, and orthosomnia itself has no official diagnostic checklist — a 2024 study of 523 adults found its apparent prevalence swung from roughly 3% to 14% depending on how strictly researchers defined it. The more durable takeaway is simpler: a tracker is a rough estimate, not a verdict. If the number on the app and how you actually feel in the morning disagree, it's worth trusting how you feel.

Further reading: Baron et al., "Orthosomnia: Are Some Patients Taking the Quantified Self Too Far?" Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine (2017), American Academy of Sleep Medicine, 2025 sleep tracking survey, and "Prevalence of Orthosomnia in a General Population Sample: A Cross-Sectional Study" (2024).
