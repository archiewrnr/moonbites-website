Sleeping With Pets: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A Mayo Clinic Proceedings study of 40 healthy adults tracked with activity monitors over seven nights found sleep efficiency was 83.1% when a dog slept in the bedroom but off the bed, versus 80.1% when the dog slept on the bed — a small but statistically significant difference.
  • A 2024 nationally representative study of 1,591 U.S. adults found 47.6% regularly co-sleep with a pet, and co-sleepers reported poorer perceived sleep quality and greater insomnia severity than people who don't.
  • The gap between what trackers measure and what owners notice is wide: most pet owners report feeling their animal helps them sleep, even in studies where the same owners' movement data shows real disruption.

Somewhere between a third and half of pet owners let their dog or cat sleep in the bedroom, and plenty let them onto the bed itself. It's a surprisingly well-studied habit, and the research splits into two different kinds of evidence that don't always agree: what people say about their sleep, and what accelerometers and polysomnography actually record while they're asleep.

The most cited objective data comes from a Mayo Clinic Proceedings study that fitted 40 healthy adults and their dogs with activity trackers for seven nights. Having a dog anywhere in the bedroom didn't meaningfully hurt sleep — participants maintained a solid 83.1% sleep efficiency, a measure of time asleep versus time in bed. But when the dog slept directly on the bed, efficiency dropped to 80.1%, a difference the researchers found statistically significant. It's a modest dip, not a dramatic one, and the study's own authors noted it was reassuring overall: having a dog in the room didn't disrupt sleep nearly as much as some had assumed, as long as the dog stayed off the mattress.

Survey data tells a more mixed story. A 2024 study in Scientific Reports surveyed a nationally representative sample of 1,591 U.S. adults and found 758 of them, or 47.6%, co-sleep with a pet. That group reported worse perceived sleep quality and higher insomnia severity scores than non-co-sleepers — though because the data was cross-sectional, it can't say whether pets caused the worse sleep or whether people already struggling with sleep or anxiety were more likely to seek out a pet's company at night. Other research has found something similar in women specifically monitored with movement sensors: dogs in the bed were linked to measurably more nighttime disturbance, yet the women themselves rarely reported being woken by their dog and generally felt the arrangement helped them feel secure and fall asleep faster.

Put together, the evidence doesn't point to a simple verdict. A pet in the bedroom appears to be a non-issue for most people's objective sleep; a pet in the bed introduces a real but usually small cost in continuity, which many owners either don't notice or decide is worth the comfort trade-off. If you wake up tired more often than not, the Sleep Foundation's practical suggestion is simple: keep the bedroom, lose the bed — a pet nearby without fur on the pillow.

Further reading: Patel et al., "The Effect of Dogs on Human Sleep in the Home Sleep Environment," Mayo Clinic Proceedings (2017), "Co-sleeping with pets, stress, and sleep in a nationally-representative sample of United States adults," Scientific Reports (2024), and "Sleeping With Pets: Benefits, Risks, and Tips," Sleep Foundation.
