Does the Full Moon Really Affect How You Sleep?

Quick takeaways
  • A 2013 lab study by Christian Cajochen's team reanalyzed sleep data from 33 volunteers who had no idea the moon was being studied, and found that around the full moon, deep-sleep (delta) brain activity fell by about 30%, it took roughly 5 minutes longer to fall asleep, and total measured sleep time dropped by about 20 minutes.
  • A much larger 2021 polysomnography study of 852 adults in Sweden found lower sleep efficiency and more time awake during the waxing half of the lunar cycle — but only in men. Women's sleep showed no such pattern.
  • Other large analyses have found no significant lunar-sleep link at all, and no one has identified a believable mechanism — the moon's gravitational pull on a human body is far too weak to matter, so the honest summary is "mixed and unresolved," not "proven."

The idea that the moon disturbs sleep is one of the oldest pieces of folk wisdom around, and for a long time it had essentially no serious science behind it. That changed somewhat in 2013, when chronobiologist Christian Cajochen and colleagues published a study in Current Biology that went back through sleep-lab recordings collected years earlier for an unrelated project. The 33 participants had spent several days in windowless rooms under constant dim light, with no clocks, calendars, or any way to know the date — let alone the moon phase. When the researchers sorted the old data by lunar phase after the fact, nights closest to the full moon showed measurably worse sleep: less deep-sleep EEG activity, a longer time to fall asleep, roughly 20 fewer minutes of total sleep, and lower melatonin.

That finding got a lot of attention, but it came from a small, retrospective reanalysis — a format that's prone to false positives. The more convincing test came in 2021, when a team led by Christian Benedict analyzed one-night polysomnography recordings from 852 adults in Uppsala, Sweden, one of the largest such datasets ever checked against the lunar calendar. They found a real but lopsided effect: men recorded during the waxing half of the cycle had lower sleep efficiency and spent more time awake after falling asleep than men recorded during the waning half. Women's sleep showed essentially no relationship to lunar phase at all. The authors were careful to note that their design was cross-sectional, so it can show an association without proving the moon caused it.

Complicating things further, other large cohort studies looking for a lunar-sleep signal have come up empty, and the "why" remains genuinely unsettled. The moon's gravity is far too small to physically act on sleep; Cajochen has floated the idea of an ancient internal "circalunar" clock, while other sleep researchers suspect something more mundane, like extra outdoor moonlight shifting people's evening light exposure before bed. Taken together, the full-moon-and-sleep question is a real, actively studied line of research — just not one that supports a confident verdict yet. If you notice restless nights around a full moon, it may be worth tracking for a few cycles, but it isn't something to lose sleep over on its own.

Further reading: Evidence that the Lunar Cycle Influences Human Sleep (Current Biology, 2013) and Sex-specific association of the lunar cycle with sleep (Science of the Total Environment, 2021).
