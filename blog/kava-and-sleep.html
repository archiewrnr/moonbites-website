Kava and Sleep: What the Research and Safety Data Actually Show

Quick takeaways
  • A randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 61 people given 200 mg/day of kava extract WS 1490 for four weeks found statistically significant improvements in sleep quality and next-day recuperative effect, alongside reduced anxiety.
  • NCCIH notes that despite kava's long traditional use for relaxation, dedicated sleep research is still limited, and results from one standardized extract don't necessarily apply to every kava product sold as a supplement.
  • The FDA issued a consumer advisory in 2002 linking kava supplements to rare but serious liver injury, including hepatitis and liver failure, and a 2020 FDA safety memo concluded kava is not safe for recreational or relaxation use.

Kava — made from the root of Piper methysticum, a plant native to the South Pacific — has been consumed as a ceremonial, calming beverage across Pacific Island cultures for centuries. In the West it's more often sold as a capsule or tincture marketed for anxiety and sleeplessness, credited to a group of active compounds called kavalactones that act on the brain's GABA system, similarly to how some anti-anxiety medications work.

The clearest clinical evidence for sleep specifically comes from a multicenter, randomized, double-blind trial published in the Journal of Affective Disorders, which gave 61 patients with non-psychotic anxiety disorders either 200 mg a day of the standardized extract WS 1490 or a placebo for four weeks. Using a validated sleep questionnaire, the researchers found statistically significant improvements in both "quality of sleep" and "recuperative effect after sleep" in the kava group, plus a significant reduction in anxiety on the Hamilton Anxiety Scale. The authors concluded kava could be an effective option for sleep disturbance tied to anxiety.

That said, NCCIH's broader review of the evidence is more measured: research on kava for sleep on its own, apart from anxiety, remains thin, and much of what exists uses one specific pharmaceutical-grade extract rather than the wide range of kava products actually sold as supplements — which can vary a great deal in preparation and kavalactone content.

The bigger complication is safety. In March 2002, the FDA issued a consumer advisory after kava-containing supplements were linked to more than 25 reports of severe liver injury abroad — including hepatitis, cirrhosis and liver failure — plus at least one U.S. case requiring a liver transplant. The exact mechanism is still debated; some researchers point to particular kavalactone ratios, non-traditional extraction solvents, or use of the wrong plant parts, and a systematic review of aqueous kava extracts used in controlled trials found no clear evidence of liver toxicity. Even so, a 2020 FDA scientific memorandum concluded that using kava for general relaxation isn't supported as safe, and several countries have restricted or banned kava sales outright since 2002. Combining kava with alcohol or other liver-affecting substances is considered especially risky.

Kava isn't a fringe ingredient with zero evidence behind it — the WS 1490 trial is a real, well-designed result. But it's also one of the few sleep-adjacent botanicals with an actual regulatory safety warning attached, which makes it a very different conversation from something like chamomile or magnesium. Anyone considering it, especially with existing liver risk factors or regular alcohol use, should talk to a doctor first rather than treat it as a low-stakes nightly habit.

Further reading: Kava: Usefulness and Safety (NCCIH) and Clinical efficacy of kava extract WS 1490 in sleep disturbances associated with anxiety disorders (Journal of Affective Disorders, via PubMed).
