Writing a To-Do List Before Bed: What the Sleep Lab Data Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2018 Baylor and Emory study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General had 57 healthy young adults spend five minutes writing at bedtime, then monitored them overnight with polysomnography — the same lab-grade brain-wave recording used to diagnose sleep disorders.
  • People who wrote a to-do list of tasks they needed to do fell asleep significantly faster than those who wrote about tasks they had already completed.
  • The more specific the to-do list — naming exact tasks and deadlines rather than vague plans — the faster people fell asleep. Writing a more detailed completed-tasks list had the opposite effect, slowing sleep onset down.

Lying awake replaying an unfinished to-do list is common enough to have its own name in sleep research: bedtime worry about incomplete future tasks. Most advice for it is intuitive — write it down and let it go — but until fairly recently there wasn't much lab data testing whether that actually changes how fast someone falls asleep, or just feels like it should.

A team led by Michael Scullin at Baylor University, working with Emory University, tested it directly. Fifty-seven healthy adults aged 18 to 30 came into a sleep lab and spent five minutes writing before going to bed. Half were randomly assigned to write down everything they needed to remember to do over the next few days; the other half wrote about tasks they'd already completed in the days before. Researchers then recorded their sleep overnight using polysomnography, tracking brain activity to measure exactly how long it took each person to fall asleep.

The to-do list group fell asleep meaningfully faster than the completed-list group. What stood out more was the dose-response pattern within groups: the more specific and detailed someone's to-do list was — actual tasks, actual timeframes, rather than a general sense of "a lot to do" — the faster they drifted off. For the group writing about completed tasks, more detail did the opposite, drawing sleep onset out longer.

The researchers frame this as cognitive offloading: getting an unresolved task out of your head and onto paper appears to free the brain from continuing to monitor it, the way you might mentally rehearse a phone number until you've written it down. A vague list doesn't offload much, because the mind still has work left to do sorting out what the vague plan actually means — which may explain why specificity mattered so much. It's a small, low-effort experiment worth trying on a night when your head won't stop cataloguing tomorrow: five minutes with a notebook, before you turn off the light, writing down exactly what needs doing and when.

Further reading: The Effects of Bedtime Writing on Difficulty Falling Asleep: A Polysomnographic Study Comparing To-Do Lists and Completed Activity Lists (Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, via PubMed Central) and Baylor University's summary of the study.
