Intermittent Fasting and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2021 review of human trials on time-restricted eating and alternate-day fasting found sleep quality and duration, measured with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, were largely unchanged — but effects on sleep latency and efficiency were mixed across studies.
  • A 2024 systematic review reached a similar split verdict: some participants reported fewer sleep disturbances, others saw sleep efficiency drop, and the authors called for longer trials with objective sleep tracking rather than self-report.
  • A smaller 2020 analysis linked time-restricted eating to better self-reported sleep and quality of life independent of weight loss — promising, but based on small, uncontrolled pilot studies.

Intermittent fasting has become one of the most popular eating patterns in wellness culture, usually in the form of time-restricted eating (TRE) — compressing meals into a daily window, often 16:8 — or alternate-day fasting. Much of its appeal rests on claims about metabolism and weight, but a narrower question gets less attention: what does skipping breakfast, or cutting off eating hours earlier, actually do to the sleep that follows?

The clearest answer comes from a 2021 review in Nutrients that pooled human trials of TRE and alternate-day fasting in adults with overweight or obesity. Using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, a standard validated questionnaire, the reviewers found no consistent change in overall sleep quality or total sleep time. Where the picture got murkier was sleep latency (how long it takes to fall asleep) and sleep efficiency (how much of time in bed is actually spent asleep): one trial showed both getting worse under fasting, while others showed no effect at all. A 2024 systematic review in Frontiers in Nutrition came to much the same split conclusion — some people's sleep disturbances eased, others' sleep efficiency slipped — and explicitly flagged that most of this research relies on short durations and self-reported sleep diaries rather than objective measures like actigraphy or polysomnography.

There is a more encouraging data point, but it comes with real caveats. A 2020 secondary analysis of two pilot studies — one in office employees, one in patients with abdominal obesity — found TRE was associated with improved sleep quality and health-related quality of life, and that the sleep benefit didn't simply track with how much weight people lost. That's a plausible mechanism worth taking seriously, but the studies were small, uncontrolled, and designed to look for feasibility rather than prove causation; the authors themselves called for larger randomized trials before drawing firm conclusions.

Put together, the honest summary is that intermittent fasting doesn't reliably wreck sleep for most people, but it also isn't a proven sleep intervention — results seem to depend heavily on the individual, the fasting protocol, and how late the eating window runs relative to bedtime. If you're curious, the practical test is simple: track how you actually sleep for a week or two before assuming the schedule is either helping or hurting.

Further reading: Intermittent Fasting and Sleep: A Review of Human Trials (Nutrients, 2021), systematic review of fasting and sleep outcomes (Frontiers in Nutrition, 2024), and secondary analysis of time-restricted eating pilot studies (2020).
