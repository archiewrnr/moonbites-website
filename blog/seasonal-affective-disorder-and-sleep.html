Seasonal Affective Disorder and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • In a symptom-questionnaire study of 293 people with winter-pattern seasonal affective disorder (SAD), 80% reported oversleeping (hypersomnia) as their main sleep complaint, compared with just 10% who reported insomnia.
  • A 2025 analysis of roughly 73 million nights of objective sleep-tracker data found people sleep about 15 to 20 minutes longer in winter than in summer, and the effect grows stronger the farther someone lives from the equator.
  • NIMH's leading explanation is the "phase-shift hypothesis": shrinking morning daylight pushes the internal circadian clock out of sync with the sleep-wake schedule, which is why light therapy soon after waking is a frontline treatment for winter-pattern SAD.

As the days shorten heading into fall and winter, sleep quietly shifts for almost everyone — and for a smaller group of people, it shifts enough to become seasonal affective disorder, a recognized subtype of depression tied to the time of year. The sleep pattern that shows up with winter-pattern SAD is distinctive: rather than the insomnia usually associated with depression, most people describe sleeping more than usual and still feeling exhausted.

That distinction comes from one of the original symptom studies of the condition, which surveyed 293 people diagnosed with winter-pattern SAD. Eighty percent reported hypersomnia — oversleeping, difficulty getting out of bed, excessive daytime drowsiness — as their primary sleep complaint, while only 10% reported insomnia, with the remainder reporting both or neither. That lopsided split is part of why SAD is often described clinically as looking more like atypical depression than classic depression, alongside its other hallmark symptoms: low energy, carbohydrate cravings, weight gain, and social withdrawal, all of which tend to resolve on their own once the days lengthen again in spring.

The seasonal sleep shift isn't confined to people with a SAD diagnosis, though — it's just milder in the general population. A 2025 analysis in the journal Sleep drew on roughly 73 million nights of data collected between 2020 and 2023 from an FDA-cleared under-mattress sensor, one of the largest objective sleep datasets ever assembled. On average, people slept about 15 to 20 minutes longer in winter than in summer, and the size of that seasonal swing increased the farther a person lived from the equator — consistent with daylight length, rather than temperature or holiday schedules, being the main driver.

The mechanism behind both the mild general effect and the more severe SAD pattern is thought to be the same. According to NIMH's phase-shift hypothesis, shorter and later-arriving morning daylight in fall and winter delays the internal circadian clock relative to the externally imposed sleep-wake schedule — so the body's rhythm, including the overnight melatonin signal, is still running on "earlier" time when the alarm goes off. That mismatch is the rationale behind light therapy, which typically means sitting near a bright light box (around 10,000 lux) for 20 to 30 minutes shortly after waking; it's recommended by NIMH and the Mayo Clinic as a first-line treatment for winter-pattern SAD, precisely because it supplies the morning light signal the season has taken away.

Further reading: "Seasonal Affective Disorder," National Institute of Mental Health, Anderson et al., "Sleep in fall/winter seasonal affective disorder: effects of light and changing seasons," Journal of Psychosomatic Research (1994), and "Variations in sleep duration and timing: weekday and seasonal variations in sleep are common in an analysis of 73 million nights from an objective sleep tracker," Sleep (2025).
