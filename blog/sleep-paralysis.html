Sleep Paralysis: What Causes It and Why It Feels So Frightening

Quick takeaways
  • Sleep paralysis is a timing mismatch, not a malfunction: consciousness returns while the muscle atonia of REM sleep is still switched on, so the mind wakes up a little before the body does. A 2011 meta-analysis pooling 36,533 people across 35 studies found about 7.6% of the general population has experienced it at least once — rising to 28.3% among students.
  • The paralysis itself is the same GABA- and glycine-driven brainstem circuit that keeps everyone from physically acting out their dreams every night. An episode happens when that circuit stays switched on a little too long during the transition into or out of REM sleep.
  • The clearest risk factors are ordinary rather than exotic: sleeping on your back, an irregular sleep schedule, sleep deprivation, and anxiety-spectrum conditions including PTSD and panic disorder are all consistently linked to more frequent episodes.

Waking up unable to move — sometimes with a felt sense of a presence in the room, pressure on the chest, or vivid, dreamlike hallucinations — is one of the more unsettling ordinary experiences the body can produce. It's also old news: cultures around the world have folklore for it, from the "old hag" of Newfoundland to the sleep demons described in medieval European and East Asian texts. Modern sleep science has a considerably less dramatic explanation.

During normal REM sleep, the brain deliberately paralyzes the body. Neurons in the ventral medulla release GABA and glycine onto motor neurons in the spinal cord, switching off the muscles so a person can't physically act out what they're dreaming. A 2024 review in the journal Nature and Science of Sleep, "Recent Insights Into Sleep Paralysis: Mechanisms and Management," describes sleep paralysis as what happens when awareness returns before that circuit has disengaged — the mind resurfaces into wakefulness while the body is still, briefly, running REM's shutdown program.

It's more common than the folklore suggests. The most widely cited estimate comes from a 2011 systematic review and meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews by Sharpless and Barber, which pooled 35 studies and 36,533 participants: roughly 7.6% of the general population reported at least one lifetime episode, climbing to 28.3% among students and 31.9% among psychiatric patients — groups where irregular sleep and elevated anxiety are both more common. The Sleep Foundation notes the clearest behavioral risk factors line up with that pattern: sleeping on your back, an irregular or disrupted sleep schedule, and anxiety-spectrum conditions such as PTSD and panic disorder. Abruptly stopping alcohol or certain antidepressants can also trigger it, by causing a rebound surge of REM sleep.

An isolated episode, however frightening, generally isn't dangerous and doesn't need treatment on its own — most people find the muscles release within seconds to a couple of minutes. Where it helps to see a doctor is if episodes are frequent, distressing, or paired with excessive daytime sleepiness or sudden muscle weakness when laughing or startled, which can point toward narcolepsy. For everyone else, the research points to the same unglamorous fix that helps most sleep problems: a more consistent sleep schedule, enough total sleep, and, where anxiety is a driver, addressing that directly.

Further reading: Sleep Paralysis: Symptoms, Causes, and Treatment (Sleep Foundation) and Recent Insights Into Sleep Paralysis: Mechanisms and Management (Nature and Science of Sleep, 2024).
