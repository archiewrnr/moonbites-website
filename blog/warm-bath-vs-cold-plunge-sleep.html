Warm Bath or Cold Plunge? What Actually Helps You Fall Asleep

Quick takeaways
  • A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that a warm shower or bath, taken 1-2 hours before bed at roughly 104-109°F, meaningfully shortens the time it takes to fall asleep.
  • The mechanism is the same one behind "cool bedroom" advice: warm water dilates blood vessels near the skin, which paradoxically helps your core temperature drop faster once you're out of the water — and that drop is what triggers sleepiness.
  • Cold-water immersion has a much thinner and more mixed evidence base for sleep specifically, and cold exposure too close to bedtime can raise alertness rather than lower it, so timing matters far more than for a warm bath.

Cold plunges have become a wellness fixture, often pitched as a fix for everything from mood to inflammation to sleep. The pre-bed ritual with the strongest sleep evidence behind it is actually the opposite: a warm shower or bath.

The clearest data point comes from a 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis published in Sleep Medicine Reviews, which pooled 13 studies and found that passive body heating from a warm shower or bath — scheduled roughly 1 to 2 hours before bedtime, at a water temperature around 104-109°F, for as little as 10 minutes — reliably improved sleep quality and shortened sleep onset latency, the time it takes to actually fall asleep. The mechanism tracks with what's known about thermoregulation: warming the skin dilates blood vessels in the hands and feet, which increases the rate at which the body can dump heat once you step out. Core temperature then falls faster than it would have otherwise, and that drop is one of the body's strongest internal signals that it's time to sleep.

Cold-water immersion doesn't have anywhere near the same depth of sleep-specific evidence. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis pooling data from cold-water immersion studies found some signal for improved sleep quality and reduced stress, along with better heart rate variability, but the authors were candid that the evidence base is still limited — few high-quality randomized trials, small samples, and benefits that faded within hours to months in some of the included studies. There's also a plausible reason cold exposure could backfire close to bedtime: the cold-shock response activates the sympathetic nervous system, the same "alert" pathway you're trying to wind down before sleep, which is the opposite of what a warm bath does.

None of this makes cold plunges bad — the research on daytime cold exposure for other outcomes is a separate question. But if the specific goal is falling asleep faster tonight, the better-supported move is boring: a warm shower an hour or two before bed, not a cold one right before it.

Further reading: Before-bedtime passive body heating by warm shower or bath to improve sleep: A systematic review and meta-analysis (Sleep Medicine Reviews, via PubMed) and Effects of cold-water immersion on health and wellbeing: A systematic review and meta-analysis (PLOS One).
