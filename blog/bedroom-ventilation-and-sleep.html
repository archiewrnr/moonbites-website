Why Cracking a Window Might Actually Help You Sleep

Quick takeaways
  • In a 2024 study in Building and Environment, 36 young adults slept in a controlled bedroom where CO2 was held near 750, 1,000, or 1,300 ppm — sleep efficiency was measurably lower and time spent awake was higher at the two elevated levels.
  • A separate 2023 field study of real Danish bedrooms, published in Science of the Total Environment, found that nights with poorer ventilation came with significantly less time in deep sleep than better-ventilated nights.
  • Both research groups point to the same low-effort fix: airing out the bedroom — a cracked window, an open door, or a fan — before or during sleep, rather than any device or supplement.

Most sleep hygiene advice covers light, noise, and temperature. Air is mentioned less often, but a closed bedroom door overnight is basically a small sealed box: with every exhaled breath, carbon dioxide concentration climbs, sometimes reaching levels three or four times higher than outdoor air by morning. Researchers at the Technical University of Denmark (DTU) have spent the past decade asking whether that buildup actually changes how well people sleep, not just how stuffy a room feels.

Their most controlled test came in 2024, published in Building and Environment. Thirty-six young adults slept in an experimental bedroom across separate nights, each time with ventilation adjusted so CO2 held steady around a different target — roughly 750 ppm (good ventilation), 1,000 ppm, or 1,300 ppm. Using actigraphy to track sleep, the researchers found sleep efficiency dropped and time awake during the night increased at both 1,000 and 1,300 ppm compared with the 750 ppm condition — a dose-dependent pattern suggesting the air itself, not just comfort, was doing something measurable.

A companion line of research took the question out of the lab. A 2023 cross-sectional study in Science of the Total Environment monitored real bedrooms in Denmark and compared nights with better versus worse ventilation. On the poorly ventilated nights, participants spent significantly less time in deep sleep — the stage most tied to physical restoration — even though the researchers didn't find a clear effect on next-day cognitive performance. Both teams are careful to note their samples are small and mostly young, healthy adults, and that CO2 is likely acting as a convenient marker for stale, under-ventilated air rather than a toxin in its own right at these concentrations.

None of this calls for a bedroom CO2 monitor. The practical thread running through both studies is simple: a bedroom door or window left open a crack, or a fan keeping air moving, is enough to keep CO2 well under the roughly 1,000 ppm threshold where these studies start seeing worse sleep — a small habit sitting right alongside the usual advice to keep the room cool, dark, and quiet.

Further reading: The Effect of CO2-Controlled Bedroom Ventilation on Sleep (Building and Environment, via DTU Orbit) and A Cross-Sectional Field Study of Bedroom Ventilation and Sleep Quality (Science of the Total Environment, via DTU Orbit).
