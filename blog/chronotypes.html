Night Owl or Morning Lark? What the Genetics of Chronotype Actually Show

Quick takeaways
  • A 2019 genome-wide study of 697,828 people identified 351 genetic locations linked to being a "morning person" — up from just 24 known before — showing chronotype has a real, measurable inherited component.
  • In a subgroup of 85,760 people tracked with wrist activity monitors, those carrying the most morningness-linked gene variants had sleep timing that ran about 25 minutes earlier, on average, than those carrying the fewest.
  • Using Mendelian randomization, the same study found that genetically-driven morningness was causally linked to better self-reported wellbeing and lower risk of depression and schizophrenia, but had no effect on BMI or type 2 diabetes risk.

"Night owl" and "morning lark" get treated like personality quirks or, worse, character flaws — as if staying up late is a discipline problem waiting to be fixed with enough willpower. The genetics tell a more specific story. Chronotype, your natural inclination toward earlier or later sleep timing, is shaped in real part by inherited biology, not just habit or laziness.

The clearest evidence comes from a 2019 genome-wide association study published in Nature Communications, which analyzed genetic data from 697,828 people drawn from UK Biobank and 23andMe. It identified 351 genetic loci associated with morningness, a dramatic jump from the 24 that had been confirmed before. Many of these loci sit near genes already known to be involved in circadian clock regulation, and in genes expressed in the retina, hypothalamus and pituitary — the tissues that translate light exposure into the internal timing signals that set your sleep-wake clock.

Genetics doesn't just correlate with a questionnaire answer, either. In a subset of 85,760 participants who wore activity monitors, the people carrying the most morningness-associated gene variants had measured sleep timing about 25 minutes earlier, on average, than those carrying the fewest. That's a modest shift, not a rigid schedule dictated entirely by DNA — chronotype is also shaped by age, light exposure and daily routine — but it confirms the biological signal is real and shows up in actual behavior, not just self-report.

The study also used a statistical technique called Mendelian randomization to test cause and effect, rather than just correlation. It found that a genetic tendency toward morningness was causally associated with better self-reported wellbeing and a lower risk of depression and schizophrenia. It found no effect on BMI or type 2 diabetes risk, which matters because chronotype and metabolic health are often lumped together in less careful reporting. None of this means being a night owl is unhealthy by default — it means fighting a strongly evening-leaning biology by forcing an early schedule may cost more than it's usually given credit for. Where your schedule allows it, working with your natural timing, rather than against it, is the more evidence-backed approach.
