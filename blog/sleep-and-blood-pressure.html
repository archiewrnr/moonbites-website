Sleep and Blood Pressure: What the Research on Heart Health Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2024 meta-analysis pooling 16 cohort studies and just over 1 million people across six countries found short sleep duration raised the risk of developing hypertension by about 7%, rising to 11% for people sleeping less than five hours a night.
  • Blood pressure is supposed to dip 10-20% during healthy sleep — a pattern called "nocturnal dipping" — and chronically short or disrupted sleep appears to blunt that overnight recovery.
  • In 2022, the American Heart Association added healthy sleep duration to its "Life's Essential 8" checklist, putting it on equal footing with diet, exercise, and blood pressure itself as a core measure of cardiovascular health.

Blood pressure advice usually starts with sodium, exercise, and weight — sleep rarely comes up in the same breath. That's changed in the last few years, as large-scale studies have connected sleep duration to hypertension risk closely enough that a major cardiology body now treats sleep as a core vital sign in its own right.

The clearest evidence comes from a systematic review and meta-analysis published in PLOS One in 2024, which pooled data from 16 cohort studies conducted between 2000 and 2023, covering just over one million people across six countries who had no prior history of high blood pressure. Over a median follow-up of five years, short sleep duration was associated with a 7% higher risk of developing hypertension overall, and that risk climbed to 11% among people sleeping less than five hours a night. The association held after adjusting for standard cardiovascular risk factors, and was slightly more pronounced in women than in men.

The proposed mechanism centers on something called nocturnal dipping. In a healthy sleep pattern, blood pressure normally falls by roughly 10 to 20% overnight as the body shifts out of its daytime, sympathetic-nervous-system-driven state. Researchers believe chronically short or fragmented sleep can blunt that dip, meaning the cardiovascular system spends more of the night running closer to daytime levels instead of getting its usual overnight recovery — a pattern that, repeated night after night, is thought to contribute to sustained increases in blood pressure over time.

This growing body of evidence is part of why, in 2022, the American Heart Association formally added sleep duration to Life's Essential 8, its updated checklist for cardiovascular health, replacing the older Life's Simple 7 framework. The AHA's guidance is straightforward: 7 to 9 hours a night for most adults, assessed alongside blood pressure, blood glucose, diet, physical activity, weight, cholesterol, and nicotine exposure as one of eight equally weighted components of heart health.

None of this means one short night will move the needle on your blood pressure. But the research treats consistent sleep duration as a modifiable cardiovascular risk factor in the same category as diet or exercise — one more reason a full night's sleep is worth protecting, not just for how you feel the next day.
