Sleep Regularity: Why When You Sleep May Matter as Much as How Long

Quick takeaways
  • A 2024 study in the journal SLEEP, tracking 60,977 UK Biobank participants for up to 7.8 years using accelerometer data, found the most sleep-regular participants had a 20–48% lower risk of dying from any cause than the least regular sleepers — a stronger association than total sleep duration showed.
  • Researchers measure this consistency with the Sleep Regularity Index (SRI), a 0–100 score based on how likely someone is to be asleep, or awake, at the same clock time from one day to the next. A perfectly consistent sleeper scores 100; someone with totally random timing scores near 0.
  • A 2023 consensus statement from the National Sleep Foundation's sleep timing panel concluded that regular bed and wake times are linked to better cardiovascular and metabolic health, lower inflammation, and improved mood — largely independent of how many total hours someone sleeps.

Most sleep advice starts and ends with a number: get seven to nine hours. That's still true and still matters, but a growing body of research is pointing at a second, less-discussed variable — how consistent your sleep and wake times are from one day to the next — and finding it may carry just as much weight for long-term health.

The clearest evidence comes from a 2024 study published in the journal SLEEP, led by researchers at Monash University's Turner Institute for Brain and Mental Health. Using more than 10 million hours of wrist-accelerometer data from 60,977 UK Biobank participants, the team calculated each person's Sleep Regularity Index and tracked mortality over a median follow-up of nearly eight years. People in the most regular sleep groups had a 20–48% lower risk of all-cause mortality than the least regular group, along with lower cancer and cardiometabolic mortality — and notably, regularity predicted risk more strongly than sleep duration did on its own.

The Sleep Regularity Index itself is a fairly simple idea dressed up in a precise formula: it's the probability that a person is in the same state — asleep or awake — at any two time points exactly 24 hours apart, averaged across a week of data. Someone who falls asleep and wakes up at nearly identical times every day scores close to 100. Someone whose bedtime and wake time swing around unpredictably, even if they're still logging eight hours most nights, scores much lower.

This lines up with a broader 2023 consensus statement from a National Sleep Foundation expert panel, which reviewed the existing sleep-timing literature and concluded that consistent sleep schedules are associated with better outcomes across cardiovascular health, metabolic markers like fasting glucose, inflammation, mood and depression risk, and cognitive performance — on top of, not instead of, getting enough sleep overall.

None of this means an occasional late night or a weekend lie-in is dangerous. The research is about patterns over weeks and months, not single nights. The practical takeaway researchers keep circling back to is the boring one: pick a wake-up time you can hold to most days, including weekends, and let bedtime follow from there rather than the other way around. A body clock that knows roughly when to expect sleep and wakefulness tends to run more smoothly than one that's constantly renegotiating the schedule.

Further reading: Sleep Regularity Is a Stronger Predictor of Mortality Risk Than Sleep Duration (Windred et al., SLEEP) and The Importance of Sleep Regularity: A Consensus Statement (National Sleep Foundation).
