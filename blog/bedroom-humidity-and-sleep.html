Does Bedroom Humidity Actually Affect How You Sleep?

Quick takeaways
  • A 2023 study in Sleep Health tracked 62 people's bedrooms for two weeks and found that noise, CO2, temperature, and air pollution (PM2.5) were each independently linked to lower measured sleep efficiency — but humidity was not.
  • That same humidity data was still linked to something: people's own ratings of their sleep quality and next-day sleepiness, even though the objective sleep-tracker data didn't move.
  • The Sleep Foundation's practical guidance is to keep bedroom relative humidity around 30–50%, mainly to avoid dry-air irritation and sweat that won't evaporate — a comfort target, not a proven sleep-efficiency lever.

Temperature and noise get most of the attention in sleep hygiene advice; humidity tends to come up only in passing, usually as a line about running a humidifier in winter. A 2023 study in the journal Sleep Health is one of the few to actually test it alongside the other usual suspects, using real bedrooms rather than a lab.

The researchers, drawing on baseline data from the Green Heart Project, outfitted 62 participants' bedrooms with sensors and had them wear activity trackers for two weeks, logging PM2.5, CO2, temperature, humidity, noise, and barometric pressure alongside actigraphy-measured sleep. Comparing the highest and lowest fifth of exposure for each factor, they found noise, CO2, temperature, and PM2.5 were each independently associated with reduced sleep efficiency — noise showing the largest effect, air pollution the smallest. Humidity and barometric pressure, notably, showed no such link to the objectively measured data.

What's interesting is that humidity didn't disappear from the results entirely. It still showed up as a significant predictor of how participants rated their own sleep quality and how sleepy they felt the next day on questionnaires — just not in what the sensors on their wrists actually recorded. The authors don't fully resolve that gap, but it fits a pattern seen elsewhere in sleep research: perception and physiology don't always move together, and a room that feels sticky or stuffy can color how rested someone reports feeling even when their sleep architecture hasn't obviously changed.

None of this makes humidity worth obsessing over. The standing practical advice — keep bedroom relative humidity somewhere around 30% to 50%, per the Sleep Foundation — still holds, mostly because air that's too dry can irritate the nose and throat, and air that's too damp makes it harder for sweat to evaporate and cool you down. It's a comfort target worth keeping in mind alongside a cool, dark, quiet room, rather than a factor with its own proven effect on measured sleep.

Further reading: Associations of Bedroom PM2.5, CO2, Temperature, Humidity, and Noise with Sleep (Sleep Health, 2023) and Humidity and Sleep (Sleep Foundation).
