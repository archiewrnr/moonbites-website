Sleep and the Menstrual Cycle: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Sleep itself changes measurably across a normal cycle, even without symptoms: a 2023 review in Sleep Medicine Clinics found sleep-spindle activity on the EEG rises in the luteal phase, when progesterone is elevated, even though total sleep continuity stays roughly stable in cycles without symptoms.
  • Not everyone is affected the same way. A study tracking self-reported sleep difficulty across the cycle found three distinct patterns: about 46% of women showed no consistent change, 25% had more trouble around mid-cycle, and 29% had more trouble in the days just before their period.
  • The leading explanation for premenstrual sleep trouble isn't low progesterone by itself — it's the speed of the drop. Researchers note the rate of change in late-luteal progesterone appears to matter more for sleep disruption than the absolute hormone level.

Ask a room of women whether their sleep changes over the course of a month, and most will say yes — but ask what, exactly, changes, and the answers get vague. That's partly because the honest research answer is more nuanced than "hormones ruin your sleep." Sleep does shift across the menstrual cycle, but the shift is subtler than most symptom trackers suggest, and it doesn't happen the same way for everyone.

The clearest signal comes from the EEG, not from how people feel. A 2023 review in Sleep Medicine Clinics by Alzueta and Baker found that in the postovulatory luteal phase — when progesterone is elevated — sleep-spindle activity increases relative to the follicular phase, a marker linked elsewhere in sleep research to memory consolidation and to progesterone's calming effect on the nervous system. Notably, the same review reports that in cycles without symptoms, basic sleep continuity (how long it takes to fall asleep, how often someone wakes) stays largely stable — the architecture shifts underneath without necessarily disrupting the night.

That stability doesn't hold for everyone. A study that tracked self-reported sleep difficulty across full cycles identified three separate patterns among participants: nearly half showed no consistent relationship between cycle phase and sleep trouble, a quarter reported more difficulty around ovulation, and just under a third reported more difficulty in the premenstrual, late-luteal days — the window when progesterone (and its metabolite, which acts on the same calming brain receptors as some anti-anxiety medications) drops sharply before menstruation begins. Sleep researchers increasingly point to that drop's steepness, rather than the hormone's absolute level, as the more likely driver of premenstrual sleep complaints; for a smaller group of women, that same window overlaps with premenstrual dysphoric disorder, for which insomnia is a recognized diagnostic symptom, not just an inconvenience.

The practical takeaway is less about a fix than about expectations: if sleep gets rockier in the week before a period, that's a documented, hormonally grounded pattern — not a sign that something is wrong with a person's routine. Tracking sleep alongside cycle phase for a couple of months can clarify whether the pattern applies at all, and for anyone whose premenstrual sleep loss is severe enough to disrupt daily functioning, that's a reasonable thing to raise with a doctor rather than sort out alone.

Further reading: The Menstrual Cycle and Sleep (Sleep Medicine Clinics, 2023) and Individual Differences in Self-Reported Difficulty Sleeping Across the Menstrual Cycle (PubMed).
