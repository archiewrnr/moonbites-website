Shift Work and Sleep: What Circadian Misalignment Actually Costs You

Quick takeaways
  • Shift work disorder is a recognized circadian rhythm sleep disorder, not just ordinary tiredness — research estimates it affects roughly 10% to 40% of people working night or rotating shifts, depending on the study and diagnostic criteria used.
  • A systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis linked night shift work to a 15% higher risk of cardiovascular death, a 23% higher risk of heart attack, and a 24% higher risk of coronary events compared with daytime work.
  • NIOSH's practical guidance centers on light timing — bright light early in a night shift to support alertness, and dimmer light on the commute home and before sleep — since light is the strongest cue the body clock responds to.

Shift work disorder sits in the same broad category as jet lag: a circadian rhythm sleep disorder. But instead of a one-off trip across time zones, it comes from a work schedule that repeatedly asks the body to be alert when its internal clock is pushing hard for sleep, and to sleep during the hours daylight and social rhythm say to be awake. That mismatch between the external schedule and the body's own circadian clock — not the hours themselves — is what distinguishes shift work disorder from simply being tired after a long shift.

How common it actually is depends heavily on who's being studied and how strictly "disorder" is defined, but research on people working nontraditional schedules puts the range at roughly 10% to 40%, with higher estimates among people working nights specifically versus rotating shifts. One study of hospital employees broke the condition into subtypes — some workers reported insomnia only, some excessive daytime sleepiness only, and some both — a reminder that shift work disorder isn't one uniform experience so much as a cluster of related symptoms that shows up differently person to person.

The consequences extend well past feeling foggy on a night shift. A systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of night shift work found it was associated with a 15% higher risk of cardiovascular death, a 23% higher risk of heart attack, and a 24% higher risk of coronary events compared with day work. The proposed mechanism isn't mysterious: circadian misalignment disrupts the body's normal daily rhythm of blood pressure regulation, insulin sensitivity, and inflammatory signaling, and laboratory studies have shown that even short-term misalignment measurably raises blood pressure and inflammatory markers in otherwise healthy adults.

There's no way to fully undo the tradeoff of working against your body clock, but occupational health researchers point to a few things that help. NIOSH's guidance centers on light exposure, since light is the primary cue the circadian system uses to set itself: seeking out bright light early in a night shift supports alertness when it's needed, while dimming light on the way home and in the hour before sleep — sunglasses in morning sun, blackout curtains, skipping screens — helps the body wind down despite the daylight outside. Keeping a consistent sleep-wake schedule even on days off, rather than swinging back to a "normal" schedule and then back again, is also consistently cited as reducing how disruptive the misalignment feels.

Further reading: Sleep and Work (NIOSH Science Bulletin, CDC) and Association Between Night Shift Work and Cardiovascular Disease: A Systematic Review and Dose-Response Meta-Analysis (PMC).
