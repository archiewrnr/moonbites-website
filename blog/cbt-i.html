CBT-I: Why Doctors Recommend Therapy Before Sleep Medication

Quick takeaways
  • The American College of Physicians' 2016 clinical practice guideline, published in Annals of Internal Medicine, recommends that all adults with chronic insomnia try cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) before medication.
  • CBT-I isn't a single technique — it's a structured, multi-week program built around a few core pieces, most notably sleep restriction and stimulus control.
  • A component-level analysis of the evidence found sleep restriction therapy to be the single most effective ingredient in CBT-I, while sleeping pills tend to lose their benefit once someone stops taking them.

Sleep medications are the treatment most people reach for first, but that's not what the guidelines actually recommend. In 2016, the American College of Physicians reviewed the evidence on chronic insomnia and concluded that cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia — CBT-I — should be the initial treatment offered to adults, with medication reserved for cases where CBT-I alone doesn't resolve things. Mayo Clinic and the NIH have echoed the same ordering: therapy first, drugs as a fallback.

What CBT-I actually involves surprises a lot of people, because it isn't really about relaxation exercises or vague "sleep hygiene" tips. The core components are more clinical: stimulus control, which retrains the brain to associate the bed with sleep by getting up if you're not asleep within roughly 20 minutes; sleep restriction, which temporarily limits time in bed to rebuild sleep drive and improve sleep efficiency; and cognitive restructuring, which targets the anxious thoughts about sleep that often keep insomnia going. Sleep hygiene education and relaxation training are typically included too, usually delivered over several weekly sessions with a trained clinician or, increasingly, through structured digital programs.

Not every piece pulls equal weight. A recent component network meta-analysis comparing the individual pieces of CBT-I against each other found that sleep restriction therapy produced the strongest effect on insomnia severity, with stimulus control also improving measured total sleep time. The same analysis found little independent evidence that psychoeducation, relaxation training, or cognitive therapy alone move the needle much on their own — they appear to work best as part of the full package rather than standing alone.

The other reason clinicians favor CBT-I first comes down to durability. Sleep medication can offer faster relief in the first week or two, but that benefit tends to fade once the medication stops. CBT-I takes longer to show results, but the improvement holds up after treatment ends, which is the main argument behind putting it first in line rather than treating it as a last resort.

Further reading: Management of Chronic Insomnia Disorder in Adults: A Clinical Practice Guideline From the American College of Physicians (Annals of Internal Medicine) and Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I): How It Works (Sleep Foundation).
