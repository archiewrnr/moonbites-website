5-HTP and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2024 randomized controlled trial in Clinical Nutrition gave 30 older adults 100mg of 5-HTP or nothing daily for 12 weeks; participants who started out as poor sleepers showed a significant improvement in subjective sleep quality.
  • 5-HTP is the direct metabolic precursor to serotonin, which the body can further convert into melatonin — the proposed mechanism behind any sleep effect, though the trial above was single-blinded and small.
  • Combining 5-HTP with antidepressants, migraine medication, or other drugs that raise serotonin carries a real risk of serotonin syndrome, and past manufacturing contamination issues mean sourcing matters.

5-HTP (5-hydroxytryptophan) occupies an odd spot in the sleep-supplement world: it's one step closer to serotonin than tryptophan, which makes the biochemistry straightforward, even though the human sleep evidence behind it is still thin.

The most relevant recent study comes from a team at the National University of Singapore, published in 2024 in Clinical Nutrition. Thirty adults in their mid-60s were randomized to take 100mg of 5-HTP daily or nothing, in a single-blinded design, for 12 weeks. By the end of the trial, the people who had started out as poor sleepers reported a meaningfully better global sleep score than the comparison group. The design has real limits — single-blinding leaves room for expectation effects, and a sample of thirty is small — but it's one of the few modern trials to test 5-HTP specifically for sleep rather than mood.

The underlying rationale is simple: the body makes serotonin from tryptophan in two steps, and 5-HTP is the intermediate compound in between. Supplementing with it skips the first, rate-limiting step, which is why 5-HTP reliably raises serotonin levels in a way plain tryptophan doesn't always manage. Serotonin itself doesn't cross the blood-brain barrier, but its precursor does, and serotonin is also what the pineal gland converts into melatonin at night. That chain is plausible, but it's a mechanism, not proof — raising a precursor doesn't guarantee the downstream sleep benefit researchers hope for, and most of the supporting data for 5-HTP as a sleep aid specifically is older, smaller, or mixed.

The safety side deserves equal attention. Harvard Health and other clinical sources flag that any supplement which raises serotonin can, in combination with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, or triptan migraine drugs, trigger serotonin syndrome — a rare but potentially serious reaction involving agitation, rapid heart rate, and fever. Separately, 5-HTP has a contamination history worth knowing about: a 1998 analysis by Mayo Clinic researchers found trace levels of an unidentified compound dubbed "Peak X" in commercial 5-HTP, structurally similar to a contaminant tied to the 1989 tryptophan-related eosinophilia-myalgia outbreak. The link was contested by industry groups and never conclusively resolved, but it's a reminder that, as with any amino-acid-derived supplement, sourcing and manufacturing quality aren't details to skip.

Further reading: Sutanto et al., "The impact of 5-hydroxytryptophan supplementation on sleep quality and gut microbiota composition in older adults: A randomized controlled trial," Clinical Nutrition (2024) and Harvard Health, "Is 5-HTP safe to take as a mood supplement?".
