Sleep and Hydration: The Two-Way Street Between Water and Rest

Quick takeaways
  • A 2019 study in the journal SLEEP, pooling more than 20,000 adults across the U.S. and China, found people sleeping 6 hours or less were 16–59% more likely to be inadequately hydrated than those sleeping a full 8 hours.
  • Researchers point to vasopressin, a hormone that helps the body retain water and is released later in the sleep cycle, as the likely mechanism — cutting sleep short may mean missing part of that release window.
  • The relationship runs both ways: per Sleep Foundation, dehydration symptoms like dry mouth, headache, and muscle cramps can themselves make it harder to fall and stay asleep.

Hydration and sleep tend to get filed under separate health habits — drink enough water, get enough rest — but a growing body of research suggests they're more mechanistically tangled than that framing implies. Skimping on one appears to work against the other, in both directions.

The clearest evidence comes from a 2019 study published in the journal SLEEP by researchers including Penn State's Asher Rosinger. The team analyzed hydration biomarkers from two U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) samples and one sample from the Kailuan Study in China — more than 20,000 adults in total. Using urine osmolality (how concentrated urine is) as a hydration marker, they found that adults who reported sleeping 6 hours or less a night were 16 to 59% more likely to show signs of inadequate hydration than those sleeping a full 8 hours. The effect held up across both countries, which strengthens the case that this is a physiological pattern rather than a coincidence tied to one climate or culture.

The likely explanation involves vasopressin, sometimes called antidiuretic hormone, which signals the kidneys to retain water. Its release follows the sleep cycle and is thought to ramp up later in the night, particularly during the early-morning hours. Cut sleep short — especially by waking early — and you may be clipping off part of that release window, leaving less time for the hormone to do its water-conserving work. That could explain why short sleepers, holding fluid intake steady, still tend to run more concentrated urine.

The link isn't one-directional. Sleep Foundation notes that dehydration can independently disrupt sleep: symptoms like dry mouth, headache, and leg cramps make it harder to fall or stay asleep, and adequate hydration also supports the body's normal temperature regulation, which plays into how easily you drift off. The practical takeaway isn't to chug water right before bed — that mostly just guarantees a bathroom trip at 3 a.m. — but to stay consistently hydrated across the day, so neither side of this loop is working against the other.

Further reading: Short Sleep Duration Is Associated With Inadequate Hydration (SLEEP, Oxford Academic) and Surprising Ways Your Hydration Level Affects Your Sleep (Sleep Foundation).
