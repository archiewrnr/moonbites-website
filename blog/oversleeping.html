Is It Possible to Sleep Too Much? What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis in Sleep Medicine Reviews pooled 137 prospective studies covering roughly 5.1 million people and found long sleep duration (generally more than nine hours) was associated with a 39% higher risk of all-cause mortality compared with people in the normal range, along with higher risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and obesity.
  • An earlier 2010 meta-analysis of prospective studies in the journal SLEEP found a similar pattern: long sleepers had roughly a 30% higher mortality risk than people sleeping about seven to eight hours a night.
  • Both sets of researchers are careful to note this is correlation, not proof that extra sleep itself causes harm — long sleep is also a plausible marker of underlying conditions like sleep apnea, depression, or undiagnosed illness.

Almost everything written about sleep duration focuses on the lower end — what happens if you don't get enough. Less discussed is that the relationship between sleep and health looks more like a U-shaped curve than a straight line: risk rises not just for people sleeping too little, but also for people consistently sleeping a lot.

The most comprehensive look at this comes from a 2018 paper by Jike and colleagues in Sleep Medicine Reviews, which combined data from 137 prospective cohort studies and meta-regression analysis. Compared with people sleeping in the normative range, long sleepers had a pooled risk ratio of 1.39 for all-cause mortality — meaning roughly 39% higher risk — plus significantly elevated risk of incident diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, and obesity. Interestingly, the same analysis found no significant link between long sleep and hypertension specifically, suggesting the association isn't uniform across every health outcome.

This wasn't a one-off finding. A 2010 meta-analysis in the journal SLEEP, pooling earlier prospective studies, had already reported a comparable pattern: a roughly 30% higher mortality risk among long sleepers relative to those sleeping seven to eight hours, alongside significant variation between studies.

None of this means extra sleep is dangerous in itself. Both research teams are explicit that observational data like this can't establish cause and effect. A more likely explanation, widely discussed among sleep researchers, is that long sleep often reflects something else going on — sleep-disordered breathing, depression, inflammation, or an illness not yet diagnosed — rather than being the thing doing the damage. Self-reported "time in bed" also isn't the same as restful sleep; some long sleepers may actually be getting fragmented, low-quality rest stretched over more hours.

The practical takeaway isn't to fear an occasional long night after a stressful week or a cold — that's just your body catching up. It's that a persistent need for nine-plus hours of sleep, especially alongside fatigue or low mood, is worth mentioning to a doctor rather than dismissing as harmless rest.
