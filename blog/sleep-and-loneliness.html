Sleep and Loneliness: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2018 study in Nature Communications found that a single night of sleep deprivation made people keep an approaching stranger at a noticeably wider distance — up to roughly 60% farther away — than when they were well-rested.
  • Brain scans showed sleep loss intensified activity in the brain's "near space network," the circuitry that signals when someone has gotten too close, and that activity tracked with how far people pushed others away.
  • Observers who didn't know about the sleep manipulation rated sleep-deprived participants as lonelier and less desirable to interact with — and the observers' own loneliness scores rose just from watching, suggesting the effect can spread from person to person.

Loneliness is usually framed as a social or psychological problem, something to fix with more connection, not more rest. But a body of sleep research, led by neuroscientists Eti Ben Simon and Matthew Walker at UC Berkeley, suggests the relationship runs in both directions — and that a short night of sleep can itself make a person feel, and appear, more isolated before any social situation even begins.

In the 2018 study, published in Nature Communications, 18 healthy adults were scanned once after a full night of sleep and once after a full night of total sleep deprivation. In each session, researchers filmed a stranger walking toward the participant and had them signal the moment the person felt "too close." After sleep loss, participants stopped people at a significantly greater distance — the kind of personal-space buffer usually reserved for someone much less familiar. Brain imaging during the same task showed heightened activity in regions that make up the near space network, which flags an approaching person as a potential threat, and that activity rose in step with the extra distance participants demanded. A separate online sample of 138 people, tracked over consecutive nights, found the same pattern: the worse someone's sleep the night before, the lonelier and less socially inclined they reported feeling the next day, independent of their mood.

The researchers then tested whether this was visible to others. They showed short video clips of the sleep-deprived and well-rested sessions to a separate group of observers who had no idea sleep was being manipulated. Those observers consistently rated the sleep-deprived participants as lonelier and less socially desirable, based on nothing but a brief silent clip. Stranger still, the observers' own loneliness ratings climbed after watching the sleep-deprived footage — a pattern the authors describe as a possible contagion effect, in which the social withdrawal triggered by one person's bad night can ripple outward to the people around them.

None of this means a single late night will wreck a friendship. But it's a useful reframe: feeling disconnected after a rough week of sleep may not be purely circumstantial. It can be a measurable, physiological state — one more reason that protecting sleep belongs on the same list as the habits people usually credit with keeping them socially and emotionally steady.
