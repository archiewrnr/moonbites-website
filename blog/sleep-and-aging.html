Sleep and Aging: What the Research Says Actually Changes

Quick takeaways
  • A landmark 2004 meta-analysis in the journal SLEEP, pooling 65 studies and more than 3,500 healthy people, found slow-wave (deep) sleep declines roughly 2% per decade up to age 60, alongside a loss of about 10 minutes of total sleep time per decade.
  • The same analysis found these changes largely plateau after 60 — sleep architecture in healthy older adults doesn't keep eroding indefinitely, contrary to the popular assumption.
  • The National Institute on Aging notes older adults still need about 7 to 9 hours nightly, the same as other adults; what changes most is timing and continuity, not the underlying sleep requirement.

There's a persistent myth that older adults simply need less sleep than everyone else. The research doesn't support it: the National Institute on Aging still puts the target for older adults at roughly 7 to 9 hours a night, the same range recommended for other adults. What genuinely changes with age isn't how much sleep the body requires — it's how that sleep is built, timed and held onto through the night.

The clearest picture of what changes comes from a 2004 meta-analysis published in the journal SLEEP, which pooled 65 studies and more than 3,500 healthy people across the lifespan. It found that slow-wave sleep — the deepest, most physically restorative stage — declines at a fairly steady rate of about 2% per decade up to age 60. Total sleep time drops too, by roughly 10 minutes per decade on average. What the same analysis also found, and what tends to get left out of the popular narrative, is that these changes largely level off after 60: healthy older adults' sleep architecture doesn't keep eroding indefinitely, it settles into a new, lighter baseline.

Alongside that shift in depth comes a shift in timing. The body's circadian clock tends to advance with age, nudging both bedtime and wake time earlier — a pattern researchers link partly to changes in how the body responds to the darkness cues that regulate melatonin release. That's why "early to bed, early to rise" becomes more common later in life, not because older adults are sleeping less overall, but because their internal clock is running on an earlier schedule. Sleep also tends to become more fragmented: more brief awakenings, more time spent lying awake after initially falling asleep, and lower overall sleep efficiency, meaning less of the time spent in bed is actually spent asleep.

Some of what looks like "aging" is really the accumulation of other things that happen to correlate with age. The National Institute on Aging notes that nearly 40% of adults over 65 take five or more medications, several of which — for blood pressure, pain, mood or other conditions — can affect sleep as a side effect, and that chronic health conditions become more common with age and can fragment sleep further. Sleep disorders like insomnia, sleep apnea and restless legs syndrome also grow more prevalent. That distinction matters practically: lighter, earlier, more easily interrupted sleep is a normal part of getting older on its own. New, severe insomnia, loud snoring with gasping, or persistent daytime exhaustion aren't things to write off as "just aging" — they're worth mentioning to a doctor.

Further reading: Meta-Analysis of Quantitative Sleep Parameters From Childhood to Old Age in Healthy Individuals (SLEEP) and Sleep and Older Adults (National Institute on Aging, NIH).
