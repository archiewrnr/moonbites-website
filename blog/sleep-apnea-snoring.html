Snoring vs. Sleep Apnea: How to Tell the Difference

Quick takeaways
  • Plain snoring is airflow vibrating relaxed throat tissue; obstructive sleep apnea is repeated breathing pauses of 10 seconds or more that drop blood oxygen and fragment sleep, per NHLBI.
  • Doctors classify severity with the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI): under 5 events per hour is normal, 5–14 is mild, 15–29 is moderate, and 30 or more is severe.
  • Untreated obstructive sleep apnea is linked to higher rates of high blood pressure, heart attack, and stroke — the risk climbing with severity, according to the NHLBI and American Heart Association.

Snoring is one of the most normalized sounds in a bedroom, which is part of why sleep apnea goes undiagnosed as often as it does. The two aren't the same thing, even though they're easy to confuse. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) describes snoring as the sound of turbulent air vibrating relaxed tissue in the throat and soft palate as you drift from light into deeper sleep — a mechanical issue that, on its own, doesn't necessarily interrupt breathing or lower blood oxygen. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a different category entirely: the airway collapses enough to actually stop or significantly reduce airflow, repeatedly, for 10 seconds or longer at a time, throughout the night.

That distinction matters clinically. Mayo Clinic lists the nighttime signs of OSA as loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, and waking gasping or choking — plus daytime symptoms plain snoring rarely causes, including excessive sleepiness, morning headaches, a dry mouth, and trouble concentrating. Sleep specialists quantify severity with the Apnea-Hypopnea Index, or AHI, which counts apneas and hypopneas (partial airflow reductions) per hour of sleep during a sleep study: under 5 is considered normal, 5 to 14 is mild, 15 to 29 is moderate, and 30 or higher is classified as severe.

It's also not a simple one-to-one relationship. Plenty of loud, chronic snorers have completely normal breathing and oxygen levels — no OSA at all. And some people with confirmed sleep apnea snore only lightly, or not in a way anyone around them would flag as unusual, which is part of why NHLBI estimates the disorder remains substantially underdiagnosed. Being overweight, having a narrowed airway, or certain jaw structures are among the most common risk factors, and the condition is diagnosed more often in men, though the gap narrows after menopause.

Why it's worth taking seriously beyond the noise: the NHLBI and the American Heart Association both link untreated OSA to higher rates of high blood pressure, coronary artery disease, heart attack, and stroke, with risk generally rising alongside AHI severity. None of this means every snorer needs a sleep study. But snoring paired with witnessed breathing pauses, gasping awakenings, or daytime sleepiness that doesn't track with how many hours you're actually in bed is the combination worth mentioning to a doctor — the standard next step is an overnight sleep study, and treatments like CPAP therapy are well established once OSA is confirmed.

Further reading: What Is Sleep Apnea? (NHLBI, NIH), Obstructive Sleep Apnea: Symptoms and Causes (Mayo Clinic) and Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) and Sleep Apnea (Sleep Foundation).
