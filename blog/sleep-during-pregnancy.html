Sleep During Pregnancy: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Insomnia becomes steadily more common as pregnancy progresses — Sleep Foundation research puts it at around 25% in the first trimester, rising past 40% by the third, with roughly 60% of people reporting disrupted sleep in the final weeks.
  • Rising estrogen and progesterone, alongside physical discomfort, frequent urination, and anxiety about labor, are the main drivers — and observational research has linked poor sleep in pregnancy to higher blood pressure, gestational diabetes, and preterm birth, though these are associations, not proof of direct cause.
  • In the third trimester, falling asleep on your side (either side) is the evidence-backed recommendation: a large meta-analysis found going to sleep on the back was linked to roughly double or more the odds of late stillbirth — but the popular claim that the left side specifically beats the right isn't well supported by the same data.

Sleep tends to get harder, not easier, as pregnancy goes on. Sleep Foundation research describes insomnia symptoms affecting roughly a quarter of people in the first trimester, climbing past 40% by the third, with an estimated 60% experiencing some form of disrupted sleep in the last stretch before delivery. That's a meaningful shift from the fatigue most people associate with early pregnancy — the problem later on isn't wanting to sleep less, it's that sleep itself becomes harder to get and keep.

Part of that is hormonal. Estrogen and progesterone both rise substantially over the course of pregnancy, and progesterone in particular can fragment sleep architecture even as it makes people feel drowsy during the day. The rest is mechanical and situational: a growing belly makes comfortable positions harder to find, frequent bathroom trips interrupt the night, heartburn and leg cramps become more common, and anxiety about labor and the transition to parenthood adds its own layer of wakefulness. None of this is a personal failing — it's a fairly predictable pattern that shows up across the research.

What makes pregnancy sleep worth taking seriously isn't just comfort. Observational studies have associated significant sleep deprivation and disrupted sleep during pregnancy with higher rates of gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, and preterm birth. These are correlations from population data, not controlled proof that bad sleep alone causes any one of them — pregnancy complications have many contributing factors — but the pattern is consistent enough that clinicians increasingly treat sleep as a genuine piece of prenatal health, not just an inconvenience.

One place the evidence is more specific: sleep position in the third trimester. A large individual-participant-data meta-analysis pooling multiple studies found that going to sleep on the back in late pregnancy was associated with roughly double to eight-fold higher odds of late stillbirth compared with going to sleep on the side. That's the basis for the now-common advice to fall asleep on your side, including during naps. What the same body of research does not clearly support is the popular idea that the left side is meaningfully safer than the right — one earlier study suggested it, but a larger follow-up study found no measurable difference between the two sides. The takeaway researchers actually stand behind is simpler: side, not back, and either side is fine.

Further reading: Pregnancy & Sleep (Sleep Foundation) and An Individual Participant Data Meta-analysis of Maternal Going-to-Sleep Position and the Risk of Late Stillbirth (eClinicalMedicine).
