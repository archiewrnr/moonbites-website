White Noise and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2021 systematic review in Sleep Medicine Reviews pooling 38 studies found the certainty of evidence that continuous background noise improves sleep to be very low — researchers still can't say whether it works by masking outside noise or through some more direct effect on the brain.
  • Short-term use wasn't linked to meaningful adverse effects in the studies reviewed, so trying it is low-risk even though the proven benefit is thin.
  • A 2014 study in Pediatrics found several sound machines exceeded 85 decibels at close range — a volume that would require hearing protection over an 8-hour workplace shift — a reminder that how loud matters as much as whether it works.

Somewhere along the way, the whir of a box fan or a phone app playing static became one of the most common bedtime rituals around. It's an easy habit to adopt and a hard one to question, mostly because it feels like it's working. But "feels like it's working" and "measurably improves sleep" are different claims, and it's worth checking what happens when researchers actually try to separate them.

The most thorough attempt so far is a 2021 systematic review published in Sleep Medicine Reviews, which pooled 38 studies examining continuous white or broadband noise and sleep. Its conclusion was blunt: the certainty of evidence that this kind of noise improves sleep is very low. The review notes that white noise is generally thought to work, if it works at all, by masking disruptive environmental sounds — a creaking floor, traffic outside, a partner's breathing — rather than by directly altering brain activity the way a sedative would. That's a meaningfully more modest claim than "white noise helps you sleep," and it explains why results across individual studies have been so inconsistent: a masking effect should only help in noisy environments, and do little to nothing in an already-quiet bedroom.

That inconsistency doesn't mean the habit is harmful, and it doesn't mean it can't help you personally — sleep researchers are generally comfortable with people continuing things that feel effective and carry little downside. What the review does support is tempering the confidence behind the claim: the evidence base is thin, not damning, and largely reflects how hard it is to isolate one variable in something as individual as sleep.

The more concrete, well-documented caution in this area isn't about whether noise helps — it's about how loud it is. A 2014 study in Pediatrics measured sound output from 14 commercial infant sleep machines at full volume and found every one exceeded 50 decibels, the recommended limit for hospital nurseries, at a distance of about a foot. Three of them topped 85 decibels — the threshold at which, over an 8-hour shift, occupational safety guidelines require hearing protection for adults. The researchers' practical advice was straightforward: keep volume low, and keep the device as far from your head as the room allows. It's a detail built for infant sound machines, but the physics of a speaker running all night next to your pillow doesn't change once you're an adult.

Further reading: Noise as a sleep aid: A systematic review (Sleep Medicine Reviews) and Infant Sleep Machines and Hazardous Sound Pressure Levels (Pediatrics).
