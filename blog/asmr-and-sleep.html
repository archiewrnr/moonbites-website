ASMR and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • ASMR — the tingling, calming sensation some people feel while listening to whispering, tapping, or other soft sounds — got its first real lab test in a 2018 University of Sheffield study, which found that people who experience it had heart rates roughly 3 beats per minute lower while watching ASMR videos than people who don't.
  • The same study found higher skin conductance (a marker of physiological arousal) alongside the lower heart rate, plus more self-reported calm and less stress — a combination the researchers compared to the relaxed-but-engaged state seen with music or mindfulness.
  • No study has yet used objective overnight sleep monitoring to confirm ASMR actually improves sleep; the sleep-specific evidence is still self-reported, and roughly half of people feel little or nothing from ASMR triggers at all.

ASMR — short for "autonomous sensory meridian response" — started as an online community label around 2010 for a sensation people struggled to name: a pleasant, tingling feeling that usually starts at the scalp and spreads down the neck in response to specific soft sounds or gentle, personal-attention visuals. Whispering, tapping, hair brushing, crinkling paper, and slow, deliberate movements are among the most commonly reported triggers, and the genre has since grown into one of the largest categories on YouTube, with millions of people using it specifically as a bedtime wind-down.

The first peer-reviewed physiological test of ASMR came in 2018, when Giulia Poerio and colleagues at the University of Sheffield published a two-part study in PLOS ONE. In the lab portion, 110 participants — about half of whom identified as ASMR experiencers — watched ASMR videos while researchers measured heart rate and skin conductance. Experiencers showed an average heart-rate drop of about 3.14 beats per minute relative to non-experiencers watching the same clips, alongside increased skin conductance and higher self-reported tingling, calmness, and connectedness, with lower stress and sadness. The combination of a slower heart rate and heightened skin conductance is unusual, and the authors read it as evidence of a relaxed-yet-attentive state, rather than ASMR simply being drowsiness or boredom.

That study is a meaningful data point, not proof that ASMR improves sleep. The sample was self-selected, modest in size, and measured short-term reactions in a lab, not what happens overnight in bed. An earlier 2015 survey of 475 regular ASMR viewers (Barratt & Davis, PeerJ) found many used it specifically to help with sleep onset and stress, and reported temporary relief from low mood — but that's self-report from an already-interested group, not a controlled trial. As the Sleep Foundation's own review of the evidence puts it, no study has yet paired ASMR with objective overnight sleep tracking, so claims about deeper or longer sleep remain unverified.

Put plainly: ASMR has real, measurable calming effects on the body for people who experience it, which is a reasonable mechanism for easier sleep onset — but the leap from "calms some people in a lab" to "proven sleep aid" hasn't been made yet. It's also not universal; survey data suggests close to half of listeners feel nothing from it. If a whispered video or a tapping sound helps you unwind, there's no evidence it's risky, and a plausible physiological reason it might work. If it does nothing for you, that's normal too.

Further reading: More Than a Feeling: Autonomous Sensory Meridian Response (ASMR) Is Characterized by Reliable Changes in Affect and Physiology (PLOS ONE, 2018) and ASMR and Sleep (Sleep Foundation).
