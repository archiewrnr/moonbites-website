Sleep Divorce: What the Research Actually Says About Sleeping Apart

Quick takeaways
  • A 2025 American Academy of Sleep Medicine survey found 31% of U.S. adults now practice a "sleep divorce" — sleeping in a separate bed or room from their partner at least some nights — with 35-to-44-year-olds most likely (39%) and adults 65 and older least likely (18%).
  • Snoring (57%) and mismatched schedules (56%) were the top reasons couples gave for sleeping apart, according to the same AASM survey.
  • Sleep Foundation survey data found more than half of people who started sleeping separately reported better sleep quality, and on average logged around 37 more minutes of sleep per night than when sharing a bed.

"Sleep divorce" is a needlessly dramatic name for something fairly ordinary: two people deciding that sharing a bed every single night isn't actually working for their sleep, and doing something about it. The term implies a marriage in trouble, but the data tells a more mundane story — it's usually a practical fix for a physical problem, not a symptom of a relationship one.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2025 survey, fielded in June of that year, found that nearly a third of U.S. adults have arranged separate sleeping spaces with a partner, whether that means another bed in the same room or a different room entirely. The habit was most common among adults in their mid-30s to mid-40s and least common among people 65 and older. Snoring topped the list of reasons, followed closely by partners simply keeping different schedules — one an early riser, the other a night owl, each disturbing the other's sleep by just existing on a different clock. AASM spokesperson Dr. Seema Khosla has pointed out why this matters beyond comfort: poor sleep worsens mood and makes people more likely to argue with their partners, so an unresolved sleep mismatch can end up straining the relationship it's supposedly protecting.

That's the argument in favor of sleeping apart, and separate survey data from the Sleep Foundation backs it up on the sleep side specifically: people who started sleeping separately reported meaningfully better sleep quality, and gained more sleep overall compared to when they shared a bed. For a restless sleeper paired with a light one, or a snorer paired with someone who wakes at the slightest sound, that's not a small effect — sleep loss compounds night after night in a way that's easy to underestimate.

None of this means sharing a bed is a problem to be solved, or that every couple should aim for separate rooms. Plenty of people sleep fine next to a partner, and the physical closeness itself has its own value that a spreadsheet of sleep-quality minutes doesn't capture. The more useful takeaway is that treating separate sleep spaces as a legitimate option — rather than a last resort or a bad sign — lets couples actually solve the thing keeping them both up, instead of quietly tolerating it for years.
