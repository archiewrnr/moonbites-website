Jet Lag: Why Flying East Hits Harder Than Flying West

Quick takeaways
  • The Sleep Foundation classifies jet lag as a circadian rhythm sleep disorder that typically follows crossing three or more time zones, with recovery generally taking about a day per time zone crossed.
  • For roughly 75% of travelers, jet lag is more severe heading east than west, according to the Sleep Foundation — a reflection of how the body's internal clock handles delays versus advances differently.
  • Harvard Medical School's guidance for resetting the circadian clock centers on strategically timed light exposure, with the recommended timing flipped depending on which direction you're flying.

Jet lag happens when your internal clock stays anchored to the time zone you left while the light, meals and activity around you are now on a different schedule. The Sleep Foundation notes it typically shows up after crossing three or more time zones, with common effects including trouble sleeping, daytime sleepiness, poor concentration and general malaise. Mayo Clinic adds that gastrointestinal upset and mood changes are common too, and that recovery tends to take roughly a day for each time zone crossed — so a nine-hour shift can mean the better part of a week feeling off before your rhythm fully catches up.

One detail that surprises a lot of travelers: it isn't equally hard in both directions. The Sleep Foundation reports that about three-quarters of people find eastbound travel tougher than westbound, even across the same number of time zones. The likely explanation comes down to how the body's internal clock is built. Left alone, without any external time cues, most people's circadian rhythm settles into a cycle slightly longer than 24 hours. That makes it biologically easier to delay the clock — stay up later, wake up later, the situation you're in flying west — than to advance it, which is what flying east demands: falling asleep and waking earlier than your body is ready for.

The main lever for correcting this, per Harvard Health's guidance on resetting your circadian clock, is timed light exposure. Traveling west to east, the advice is to seek out morning light at your destination and avoid bright light in the evening, which helps nudge the clock earlier. Traveling east to west, it's the opposite: afternoon and early-evening light, staying up a little later, and it's fine to sleep in a bit for the first day or two. None of this resets the clock instantly — it takes deliberate, fairly consistent timing over several days to shift meaningfully.

It's also worth separating true circadian jet lag from travel fatigue that just looks like it. Mayo Clinic points out that cabin pressure changes, high altitude and the low humidity of aircraft cabins can independently cause fatigue, headaches and GI discomfort — even on flights that don't cross a single time zone. Staying hydrated and pacing yourself on arrival won't fix a misaligned body clock, but it can keep dehydration from making an already rough adjustment period feel worse.

Further reading: How to Get Over Jet Lag (Sleep Foundation), Resetting Your Circadian Clock to Minimize Jet Lag (Harvard Health Publishing) and Jet Lag Disorder: Symptoms and Causes (Mayo Clinic).
