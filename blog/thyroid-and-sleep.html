Thyroid Function and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2024 analysis of 9,016 U.S. adults using NHANES data, published in BMC Public Health, found hypothyroidism was linked to 38% higher odds of self-reported trouble sleeping and 40% higher odds of a diagnosed sleep disorder.
  • The same study found no significant link between hypothyroidism and sleep duration itself — short or long — suggesting the real effect falls on sleep quality, not how many hours people spend asleep.
  • A 2022 study in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism found that in people with untreated Graves' disease (hyperthyroidism), higher thyroid hormone levels tracked with worse sleep quality scores and heightened "fight-or-flight" nervous system activity — and treating the condition significantly improved sleep.

The thyroid is a small, butterfly-shaped gland in the neck, but it sets the pace for metabolism, body temperature, and nervous system activity throughout the body — so it isn't surprising that having too little or too much thyroid hormone can throw off sleep. What is notable is that the two conditions disrupt sleep in almost opposite ways, through different mechanisms entirely.

A 2024 study by Ding and colleagues, published in BMC Public Health, analyzed NHANES data from 9,016 U.S. adults, among whom 8.0% had hypothyroidism (defined as TSH above 5.6 mIU/mL or current use of thyroid hormone medication). People with hypothyroidism had significantly higher odds of self-reported trouble sleeping (OR 1.38) and of having a diagnosed sleep disorder (OR 1.40) compared to those with normal thyroid function. Interestingly, sleep duration — whether short or long — showed no significant association, pointing to disrupted sleep quality rather than simple hours lost. Researchers note that slowed metabolism, weight gain, and an increased risk of obstructive sleep apnea linked to tissue swelling around the airway are plausible contributors.

Hyperthyroidism disrupts sleep through the reverse mechanism. A 2022 study in the Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism examined patients with untreated Graves' disease, the most common cause of hyperthyroidism, and found that higher free T4 (thyroid hormone) levels were independently associated with worse scores on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and correlated with markers of sympathetic nervous system activity such as faster pulse rate. In short, excess thyroid hormone appears to push the body into a low-grade state of physiological alertness that makes it harder to wind down at night. Once participants' thyroid levels were normalized through treatment, their overall sleep quality and sleep-disturbance scores both improved significantly.

None of this means every restless night points to a thyroid problem — most sleep trouble has more ordinary causes. But thyroid dysfunction is common, develops gradually, and its symptoms (fatigue, weight changes, feeling persistently too hot or too cold, anxiety) are easy to mistake for everyday tiredness. If sleep problems persist despite consistent habits, a simple TSH blood test is a reasonable thing to raise with a doctor, since thyroid-related sleep trouble is one of the more directly treatable causes once it's identified.

Further reading: Relationship between sleep abnormalities and hypothyroidism: results from NHANES 2007–2012 (BMC Public Health, 2024) and Hyperthyroidism in Graves' disease causes sleep disorders related to sympathetic hypertonia (J Clin Endocrinol Metab, 2022).
