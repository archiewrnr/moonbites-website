Orexin: The Brain Chemical That Keeps You Awake

Quick takeaways
  • Orexin (also called hypocretin) is made by a small cluster of hypothalamus neurons and was identified independently by two research teams in 1998, who gave the same discovery two different names.
  • Its main role isn't simply causing alertness — it stabilizes the switch between sleep and wakefulness and keeps REM sleep from intruding into waking hours.
  • Losing the neurons that make orexin, usually through an autoimmune process, causes narcolepsy — and blocking orexin receptors is now the basis of a newer class of FDA-approved insomnia medications.

In 1998, two research groups working independently discovered the same small family of brain peptides and gave it two different names. Neuroscientists at Scripps Research, screening for genes expressed almost exclusively in the hypothalamus, named their find "hypocretin." A separate team led by Masashi Yanagisawa and Takeshi Sakurai, searching for the natural target of an orphan brain receptor, called it "orexin," after the Greek word for appetite, once they noticed it also drove feeding behavior in rats. Both names are still used today for the same two peptides, orexin-A and orexin-B (also known as hypocretin-1 and -2).

What the peptide actually does turned out to matter far more for sleep than for appetite. Orexin-producing neurons cluster in the lateral hypothalamus but project broadly across the brain, reinforcing other wake-promoting systems — histamine, norepinephrine, serotonin — while damping down sleep-promoting circuits. Sleep researchers now describe the system less as an "alertness switch" and more as something that stabilizes whichever state the brain is already in: it keeps wakefulness from lapsing into brief, unwanted sleep intrusions, and keeps REM sleep — along with the muscle paralysis that comes with it — from bleeding into waking life.

The clearest evidence for that stabilizing role comes from what happens when the system fails. In 1999, researchers found that dogs and mice with narcolepsy carried mutations in the orexin/hypocretin genes or their receptors. In humans, though, narcolepsy almost never comes from an inherited mutation — by 2000, researchers had instead found that most people with the condition have lost the neurons that produce orexin altogether, likely through an autoimmune process that mistakenly destroys them. Once that signal is gone, the sleep-wake switch loses its stability, producing the hallmark symptoms of narcolepsy: sudden daytime sleep attacks, fragmented nighttime sleep, and cataplexy, a sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by strong emotion.

That same mechanism, run in reverse, is now a treatment target. Since orexin's job is to promote wakefulness, blocking its receptors promotes sleep — the logic behind a newer insomnia drug class called dual orexin receptor antagonists, which includes suvorexant (approved in 2014), lemborexant, and daridorexant. Unlike older sleep medications, which sedate broadly by boosting the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA, these drugs work by quieting the specific wake-promoting signal rather than sedating the brain more generally — a fairly direct line from a 1998 basic-science discovery to a mechanistically distinct class of medication two decades later.

Further reading: What Is Orexin? Its Role in Sleep and Wakefulness (Sleep Foundation) and The Regulation of Sleep and Wakefulness by the Hypothalamic Neuropeptide Orexin/Hypocretin (NIH, PMC).
