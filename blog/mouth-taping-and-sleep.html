Mouth Taping for Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A May 2025 systematic review in PLOS One pooled 10 studies and 233 patients on nocturnal mouth taping and rated every included study as poor quality — only 2 of the 10 found a statistically significant improvement in sleep apnea markers.
  • The same review flagged a real safety concern: forcing mouth closure can worsen breathing in people with nasal obstruction, and four of the ten studies excluded such patients entirely, leaving a gap in exactly who it's been shown safe for.
  • Harvard Health and sleep specialists point to one legitimate, clinician-supervised use — pairing tape with a nasal-only CPAP mask to reduce air leaks — but don't recommend it as a stand-alone fix for snoring or undiagnosed sleep apnea.

Mouth taping — placing a strip of porous tape over the lips before bed — has become one of the most visible sleep trends on social media, credited with everything from quieter nights to a more defined jawline. The logic behind it is simple enough: nasal breathing is slower and more filtered than mouth breathing, so forcing it during sleep should, in theory, mean calmer, less disrupted rest. Whether the practice actually delivers that has been surprisingly under-studied until recently.

The most comprehensive look yet came in May 2025, when researchers published a systematic review in PLOS One that searched the medical literature from February 1999 to February 2024 and identified 10 studies covering 233 patients with mouth breathing, snoring, or obstructive sleep apnea. The reviewers rated all 10 as poor quality, and only two showed a statistically significant improvement on established sleep apnea measures. Just as notably, the review raised a real safety flag: taping the mouth shut can restrict airflow in people with nasal congestion or obstruction, and four of the ten studies excluded those patients from participating in the first place — meaning the existing evidence largely skips over the people most likely to try mouth taping to relieve snoring.

A separate scoping review published around the same time, covering nine studies including four randomized controlled trials from 2019 to 2024, was somewhat more optimistic about mouth taping's "promise" for mild sleep apnea specifically — but its authors were equally clear that the research base remains extremely limited. Even the more favorable read of the evidence agrees there simply isn't much of it yet.

On the risk side, Harvard Health notes that mouth taping can cause skin irritation and, in some people, a hampered or anxious feeling around breathing rather than a calmer one. Clinicians generally advise against it for anyone who snores heavily or may have undiagnosed sleep apnea, since taping over the symptom — quieter breathing sounds — doesn't resolve an underlying airway problem, and can make it harder to notice. The one setting where tape has a more established, supervised role is alongside a nasal-only CPAP mask, where it can help reduce air escaping through the mouth for people already being treated for apnea.

None of this means mouth taping is dangerous for everyone who tries it, but the popularity of the trend has clearly outpaced the evidence behind it. For anyone whose real complaint is snoring or restless nights, a sleep evaluation is a more reliable next step than a roll of tape.
