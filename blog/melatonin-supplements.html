Melatonin Supplements: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2023 JAMA research letter tested 25 melatonin gummy brands and found 88% were inaccurately labeled, with actual melatonin content ranging from 74% to 347% of what the label claimed. One product contained no melatonin at all.
  • NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health notes melatonin's clearest evidence is for circadian timing issues like jet lag and delayed sleep-wake phase disorder — not everyday insomnia, where studies show only modest benefit.
  • Much of the research behind melatonin's effects used doses far lower than what's commonly sold — a well-cited 2005 review pointed to around 0.3 mg as most effective, versus the 3–10 mg common in store-bought bottles.

Melatonin is a hormone, not a vitamin — the pineal gland releases it in response to darkness as part of the signal that tells your body it's heading toward sleep. Taking it as a supplement is meant to nudge that signal along, which is why it tends to work best for problems of timing rather than problems of sleep itself.

That distinction shows up clearly in the research NIH's NCCIH summarizes: melatonin has reasonably good support for jet lag and for delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, where the goal is shifting an internal clock that's out of sync with the day. For general insomnia — trouble falling or staying asleep with no clock problem behind it — the evidence is weaker. Meta-analyses cited by sleep medicine guidelines describe the effect as statistically real but small: on the order of a few extra minutes of total sleep and a modest reduction in the time it takes to fall asleep, not the kind of change most people are hoping for when they reach for a bottle.

Dosing adds another wrinkle. Some of the clearest early melatonin research used doses around 0.3 mg — close to what the body produces on its own — and found that higher amounts didn't necessarily work better, just longer, since excess melatonin lingers and can be metabolized more slowly. Most commercial products sell 3, 5, or even 10 mg per dose, roughly ten to thirty times that reference amount, largely because supplement dosing isn't tied to the clinical trial literature.

That gap between the lab and the shelf became a bigger concern after a 2023 JAMA research letter tested 25 melatonin gummy products pulled from the NIH's own supplement label database. Twenty-two of the twenty-five — 88% — contained a different amount of melatonin than their label stated, with actual content ranging from 74% to 347% of the stated dose. One product had no detectable melatonin; another contained undisclosed CBD. The authors flagged this as a particular risk for children, pointing to a sharp rise in poison-control calls tied to pediatric melatonin ingestion over the prior decade, and noted that gummies — unlike regulated medications — aren't required to prove their label matches what's actually in the bottle.

None of this means melatonin is unsafe for short-term, occasional use in adults — the research generally supports that it is, within reason. It does suggest two things worth knowing: that it's a better-matched tool for circadian timing problems than for everyday insomnia, and that if you do use it, a lower, third-party-tested dose closer to what's actually been studied is a more defensible choice than whatever strength happens to be on the shelf.

Further reading: Melatonin: What You Need To Know (NIH NCCIH) and Quantity of Melatonin and CBD in Melatonin Gummies Sold in the US (JAMA, 2023).
