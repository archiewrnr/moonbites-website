Postpartum Sleep Deprivation: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2025 Rush University study tracked 41 first-time mothers with Fitbit data from a year before birth through a year after. Total sleep fell from about 7.8 hours a night pre-pregnancy to roughly 4.4 hours a day in the first postpartum week, recovering to about 7.3 hours by weeks 8–13.
  • Uninterrupted sleep recovered far more slowly: the longest unbroken stretch dropped to about 2.2 hours in week one and was still only around 4.1 hours by weeks 8–13 — well below the pre-pregnancy baseline.
  • A 2015 systematic review of 31 studies found that poor self-reported sleep during pregnancy and the postpartum period is associated with higher rates of postpartum depression, though the evidence is largely observational and doesn't settle which comes first.

Every new parent hears some version of "sleep when the baby sleeps," but most research on postpartum exhaustion has relied on surveys and diaries rather than objective tracking. A study presented at the SLEEP 2025 annual meeting, led by researchers at Rush University, used a year of Fitbit data per participant to put real numbers on how sleep actually changes after birth.

Across 41 first-time mothers, total daily sleep dropped sharply in the first week after delivery — from an average of about 7.8 hours a night before pregnancy to roughly 4.4 hours a day — then climbed back to about 6.7 hours by weeks 2 through 7 and 7.3 hours by weeks 8 through 13. On paper, that looks like a fairly quick return to something close to normal. But the researchers found that nearly a third of participants went more than 24 hours without any sleep at all during that first week, and the measure that mattered more for how exhausted mothers felt wasn't total hours — it was how broken up that sleep was.

The longest stretch of uninterrupted sleep fell to about 2.2 hours in week one and stayed depressed for months, reaching only about 3.2 hours by weeks 2 through 7 and 4.1 hours by weeks 8 through 13 — still far short of the roughly 7-hour unbroken stretches mothers got before pregnancy. The authors argue this fragmentation, more than the total sleep number, is why new mothers can feel exhausted even once they're technically "getting enough sleep." It's worth noting this is a small, single-group study presented as a conference abstract rather than a peer-reviewed paper, so the exact figures should be treated as preliminary.

The stakes go beyond feeling tired. A 2015 systematic review of 31 studies on sleep and postpartum mental health found that poor sleep during pregnancy and the postpartum period was consistently linked to postpartum depression, and flagged sleep disruption as a plausible, low-cost target for prevention. The review is honest about its limits — most of the underlying studies relied on self-report, and researchers still disagree on whether poor sleep helps cause depression, results from it, or both feed each other. Either way, protecting even short stretches of uninterrupted sleep in the early postpartum weeks looks like a reasonable, low-risk thing to prioritize — for mood as much as for rest itself.

Further reading: Study Quantifies the Sleep Loss and Disruption Experienced by New Mothers (SLEEP 2025 / Rush University, via ScienceDaily) and The Relationship Between Sleep and Postpartum Mental Disorders: A Systematic Review (PubMed).
