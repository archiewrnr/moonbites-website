Sleep and Bone Health: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2020 analysis of 11,084 postmenopausal women in the Women's Health Initiative found that those sleeping five hours or less a night had significantly lower bone mineral density at all four measured sites than women sleeping seven hours, with higher odds of osteoporosis.
  • In a 2017 lab study, about three weeks of restricting ten men to 5.6 hours of sleep a night under a disrupted 28-hour "day" cut a key bone-formation marker by 18–28%, with no matching rise in the marker for bone breakdown — pointing to slower bone-building rather than faster bone loss.
  • A 2025 meta-analysis pooling 30 studies found that sleeping six hours or less was linked to 58% higher odds of osteoporosis and about 49% higher odds of low bone density, with six to eight hours the range associated with the least risk.

Bone looks static, but it's one of the more metabolically active tissues in the body — a constant back-and-forth between osteoblasts, which build new bone, and osteoclasts, which break old bone down. That balance shifts with age and hormones, and a growing body of research suggests it may shift with sleep duration too. The largest piece of evidence comes from the Women's Health Initiative, where researchers analyzed self-reported sleep alongside bone scans from 11,084 postmenopausal women. Those sleeping five hours or less a night had measurably lower bone mineral density at the hip, spine, neck and whole body than women sleeping seven hours, and higher rates of osteoporosis. Because the data was cross-sectional — one snapshot in time, not a controlled experiment — the authors were careful to note it shows an association, not proof that short sleep causes bone loss.

A smaller, more controlled study gets closer to a mechanism. Researchers kept ten young men on 5.6 hours of sleep a night for about three weeks, with sleep and wake times drifting through a 28-hour "day" to mimic circadian disruption. Afterward, P1NP — a marker of new bone formation — had dropped by 18% in older participants and 28% in younger ones, while CTX, a marker of bone breakdown, barely moved. That pattern suggests the body wasn't losing bone faster; it had simply slowed down building new bone. A smaller follow-up in women found a different wrinkle — CTX rose in younger women but not older ones — a reminder that researchers still call this an open question about which sleep disruptions matter most, and for whom.

Zooming out, a 2025 meta-analysis combining 30 studies found a consistent pattern: sleeping six hours or less was associated with 58% higher odds of osteoporosis and roughly 49% higher odds of low bone density, while very long sleep carried some added risk too. Six to eight hours came out as the range associated with the least risk. None of this proves that fixing your sleep will fix your bones — the underlying biology is still being worked out — but it adds skeletal health to the list of systems that quietly keep score of how consistently you sleep.

Further reading: Ochs-Balcom et al., "Short Sleep Is Associated With Low Bone Mineral Density and Osteoporosis in the Women's Health Initiative," Journal of Bone and Mineral Research (2020), Swanson et al., "Bone Turnover Markers After Sleep Restriction and Circadian Disruption," Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism (2017), and "Impact of sleep duration on bone mineral density and osteoporosis risk: A systematic review and meta-analysis," Pakistan Journal of Medical Sciences (2025).
