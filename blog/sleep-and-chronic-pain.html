Sleep and Chronic Pain: What the Research Says About Which Comes First

Quick takeaways
  • A 2024 narrative review in Clinics and Practice, led by researcher Sejal Jain and colleagues, concluded that while sleep and pain influence each other in both directions, current evidence suggests sleep impairment is the stronger predictor of the two — poor sleep tends to worsen pain more than pain worsens sleep.
  • The same review notes that at least half of people living with chronic pain conditions also have a significant sleep disturbance, and the two tend to reinforce each other rather than staying separate problems.
  • A 2013 study in the journal Pain found that a single night of total sleep deprivation was enough to produce measurable generalized hyperalgesia in healthy volunteers — a body-wide drop in pain threshold, not just next-day crankiness.

People with chronic pain are often told that better sleep "couldn't hurt," as if it were a nice-to-have alongside the real treatment. The research tells a different story: sleep looks less like a bystander in chronic pain and more like one of the more direct levers available for managing it.

The clearest summary of where the evidence stands comes from a 2024 narrative review published in Clinics and Practice. After surveying the clinical and experimental literature, the authors describe a genuinely bidirectional relationship — pain does disrupt sleep — but conclude that the sleep-to-pain direction appears stronger and more consistent across studies than the pain-to-sleep direction. They also note that sleep disturbance shows up in at least half of people with chronic pain conditions, making it one of the most common comorbidities in this population rather than an occasional side effect.

The mechanism has been demonstrated experimentally, not just observed in surveys. A 2013 study in the journal Pain put healthy adults with no history of chronic pain through a single night of total sleep deprivation, then measured their pain thresholds the next day using standardized stimuli. The result was generalized hyperalgesia — heightened pain sensitivity across the body, not localized to any one area. Researchers have since traced part of this effect to impaired conditioned pain modulation, the nervous system's own built-in ability to dampen incoming pain signals, which appears to weaken when sleep is cut short. Other work points to elevated inflammatory markers after poor sleep as a contributing factor, since several of the same signaling molecules involved in inflammation also sensitize pain pathways.

None of this suggests sleep is a cure for chronic pain, and the review is careful not to frame it that way. What it does support is treating sleep as a legitimate, modifiable target in pain management rather than an afterthought — something clinicians and patients can actually act on, alongside whatever else is being done to manage the pain itself.
