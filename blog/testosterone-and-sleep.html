Testosterone and Sleep: What the Hormone Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Testosterone follows its own overnight rhythm: levels rise after sleep onset and peak around the first REM period, a pattern driven by pulses of luteinizing hormone (LH) from the pituitary rather than by clock time alone.
  • A landmark JAMA study restricted healthy young men to under five hours of sleep a night for one week and found daytime testosterone dropped by 10-15%, with the lowest readings showing up in the afternoon.
  • The relationship runs both ways — research summarized by the Sleep Foundation links lower testosterone to reduced sleep efficiency, more nighttime awakenings, and less slow-wave sleep, so poor sleep and low testosterone can reinforce each other.

Testosterone isn't released at a steady drip throughout the day. In men, it follows a distinct overnight pattern that depends on actually being asleep, not just on what time the clock reads. Early sleep-lab research tracking hormone levels through the night found that testosterone begins climbing shortly after sleep onset and peaks around the time of the first REM period, roughly 90 minutes in. That rise is preceded by a smaller rise in luteinizing hormone, the pituitary signal that tells the testes to produce more testosterone — suggesting the brain's sleep architecture, not the sun, is setting the pace.

What happens when that architecture gets cut short was tested directly in a study published in JAMA by researchers at the University of Chicago. Healthy young men spent three nights sleeping up to ten hours in a lab, then eight nights restricted to under five hours, with blood drawn every 15 to 30 minutes across a full 24-hour period at the end of each phase. After just one week of short sleep, daytime testosterone levels were 10% to 15% lower than after the ten-hour nights, with the biggest drop showing up in the afternoon and evening hours — precisely when testosterone is normally still riding high from the previous night's sleep.

The connection isn't limited to healthy young men losing sleep on purpose. Broader research on sleep and hormones, summarized by the Sleep Foundation, associates lower testosterone with lower sleep efficiency, more frequent nighttime awakenings, reduced time spent in slow-wave sleep, and a higher rate of breathing disruptions during sleep. That pattern makes it hard to say testosterone loss is purely a downstream effect of bad sleep — it looks more like a loop, where short or fragmented sleep suppresses the hormone, and low testosterone can in turn make sleep less restorative.

None of this means one rough night meaningfully dents hormone levels — the JAMA study needed a full week of consistent restriction to show an effect. But it's a useful data point for anyone treating sleep as optional or infinitely compressible: this is a nightly biological process with a measurable output, not just a feeling of restedness.

Further reading: Effect of 1 Week of Sleep Restriction on Testosterone Levels in Young Healthy Men (JAMA) and The Link Between Sleep and Testosterone (Sleep Foundation).
