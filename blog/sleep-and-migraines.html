Sleep and Migraines: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • In a population-based study of nearly 2,700 Korean adults, insomnia was reported by 25.9% of people with migraine, compared with 15.1% of those with non-migraine headache and just 5.8% of headache-free participants.
  • In a separate population study, migraineurs who slept five hours or less a night averaged 9.8 attacks per month, versus 3.8 attacks per month among those sleeping more than five hours — even though average sleep duration was nearly identical between migraineurs and non-migraineurs overall.
  • The Sleep Foundation describes migraine and sleep as bidirectionally linked: poor or fragmented sleep can trigger an attack, and the attack's pain, light sensitivity, and nausea then disrupt the sleep that follows.

Anyone who's had a migraine knows it rarely confines itself to daytime hours — attacks can start in the middle of the night, wake a person from sleep, or leave them lying awake once the pain subsides. Headache researchers have spent the past two decades documenting just how tightly migraine and sleep are intertwined, and the relationship, it turns out, runs in both directions.

The clearest evidence comes from comorbidity data. A population-based study of nearly 2,700 Korean adults found insomnia in 25.9% of people with migraine, compared with 15.1% of those with non-migraine headache and only 5.8% of people with no headache history at all — a stepwise pattern suggesting migraine itself, not headache pain in general, carries the strongest sleep burden. Other surveys have put active insomnia symptoms in well over half of people with chronic migraine.

Sleep duration appears to matter on its own, separate from the insomnia overlap. In a separate population study, migraineurs who reported sleeping five hours or less a night averaged 9.8 attacks per month, compared with 3.8 attacks per month among those sleeping more than five hours — despite average sleep duration being nearly identical between migraineurs and non-migraineurs overall. The difference showed up specifically among people already prone to migraine, suggesting short sleep doesn't cause migraine so much as it lowers the threshold for an attack in people who already have the condition.

The Sleep Foundation describes the underlying relationship as bidirectional and mechanistic rather than coincidental: the brainstem and hypothalamic circuits that regulate sleep-wake timing overlap heavily with the pathways that process migraine pain, and chemicals implicated in both systems — including serotonin, orexin, and melatonin — shift during a migraine attack in ways that can fragment the sleep that follows. That overlap is also why clinicians increasingly treat sleep regularity as part of migraine management: keeping a consistent sleep-wake schedule won't replace medication, but it removes one of the more modifiable attack triggers.

Further reading: "Migraines and Sleep: A Bidirectional Relationship," Sleep Foundation, "Impact of migraine on the clinical presentation of insomnia: a population-based study," The Journal of Headache and Pain (2018), and Kim et al., "The effect of sleep duration in clinical features and impact of migraine: Result from a population-based study," The Journal of Headache and Pain (2013).
