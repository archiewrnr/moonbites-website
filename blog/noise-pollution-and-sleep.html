Noise Pollution and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • The WHO's 2018 Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region strongly recommend keeping nighttime road traffic noise below 45 decibels (Lnight) — a level many urban bedrooms exceed once a window is cracked open for air.
  • A 2022 update to WHO's systematic review, published in Environmental Health Perspectives, pooled decades of data and mapped clear exposure-response curves showing that as nighttime road, rail and aircraft noise rises, so does the probability of significant sleep disturbance.
  • Traffic noise disrupts sleep even when it doesn't wake you enough to remember — brief, unconscious arousals fragment deep sleep, and years of that chronic exposure have been linked to higher blood pressure and increased cardiovascular risk.

Most sleep advice focuses on things you can control at will — screens, caffeine, room temperature. Noise from outside your home is a different kind of variable: you can't switch off a nearby road or rail line, and even a well-insulated bedroom rarely blocks it out completely. It's also one of the more thoroughly studied environmental sleep disruptors, with the World Health Organization treating it as a genuine public-health issue rather than a minor annoyance.

WHO's 2018 Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region put a number on it: a strong recommendation to keep nighttime road traffic noise below 45 decibels, measured as Lnight — the average sound level between 11pm and 7am. Levels above that threshold are linked with measurable adverse health effects, not just complaints about being bothered. For context, ordinary city streets with regular traffic commonly run well above that figure at night, which is part of why the guideline reads less like caution and more like a genuine target most urban bedrooms fall short of.

A 2022 update to WHO's underlying systematic review, published in Environmental Health Perspectives, pooled data across dozens of studies and mapped exposure-response curves for road, rail and aircraft noise specifically. The pattern held consistently: as nighttime noise levels climbed, so did the probability of being highly sleep disturbed. Part of what makes this hard to notice yourself is that the disruption doesn't require waking up. Researchers describe brief "sub-awakenings" — the brain registering a passing truck or train and shifting sleep stage, or nudging heart rate and blood pressure, without ever producing a memory of being woken. You can sleep through the noise and still not get the sleep you think you did.

Do that often enough, for long enough, and the research links it to more than groggy mornings: chronic nighttime noise exposure is associated with higher blood pressure and increased cardiovascular risk, likely through the same repeated stress-hormone activation and fragmented deep sleep described above. None of this means one loud night matters much, or that living on a busy street guarantees poor health — the effects reported are gradual and population-level, not immediate. But it does make outside noise a legitimate line item alongside light and temperature when troubleshooting sleep, worth the same attention most people already give to more visible problems.

Further reading: Environmental Noise and Effects on Sleep: An Update to the WHO Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (Environmental Health Perspectives) and How Noise Can Affect Your Sleep Satisfaction (Sleep Foundation).
