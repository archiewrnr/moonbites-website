GLP-1 Drugs and Sleep Apnea: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Two phase 3 trials (SURMOUNT-OSA), published by Malhotra et al. in the New England Journal of Medicine in June 2024, gave 469 adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea weekly tirzepatide for 52 weeks; the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) fell by roughly 20–29 events per hour more than with placebo.
  • The same participants lost about 17–20% of their body weight and saw drops in systolic blood pressure and the inflammatory marker hsCRP, which lines up with the leading explanation: less tissue around the airway, not a separate drug effect on breathing itself.
  • The trials tested tirzepatide, a dual GLP-1/GIP agonist, specifically in people with obesity-related OSA; data on semaglutide-only drugs for sleep apnea is thinner, and gastrointestinal side effects were the most common complaint.

GLP-1 drugs like semaglutide and tirzepatide were built for diabetes and then obesity, but one of the more interesting findings to come out of the weight-loss-drug boom has nothing to do with the scale — it's what happens to sleep apnea along the way.

The clearest evidence comes from SURMOUNT-OSA, two 52-week, double-blind, randomized trials run at 60 sites across nine countries and published in NEJM in June 2024. One trial enrolled people who weren't using a CPAP machine; the other enrolled people who were. Across both, weekly tirzepatide cut the apnea-hypopnea index — the standard measure of how many times per hour breathing stops or nearly stops during sleep — by about 20 to 29 events per hour more than placebo, a reduction researchers described as clinically meaningful for a condition that otherwise has no approved drug treatment.

The likely mechanism is straightforward rather than novel: obstructive sleep apnea is driven in large part by excess soft tissue narrowing the airway, and tirzepatide's participants lost 17–20% of their body weight over the trial. Secondary measures moved in the same direction — systolic blood pressure and the inflammatory marker hsCRP both fell — which fits a story about reduced tissue and inflammation rather than the drug acting on breathing directly. That matters for expectations: this looks like an indirect benefit of substantial weight loss, available through a specific drug class, rather than a dedicated apnea therapy.

A few caveats are worth sitting with. The trials studied tirzepatide specifically, not GLP-1-only drugs like semaglutide, so it's not yet clear how well the finding generalizes across the drug class. Participants all had obesity alongside their sleep apnea, so the result doesn't speak to apnea in people at a healthy weight. And gastrointestinal side effects — nausea, diarrhea, constipation — were common enough that they're the main reason people stopped taking the drug in the trials. None of this replaces CPAP or a sleep medicine evaluation; it's one more data point in a fast-moving field.

Further reading: Malhotra et al., "Tirzepatide for the Treatment of Obstructive Sleep Apnea and Obesity," New England Journal of Medicine (2024) and American Diabetes Association, "SURMOUNT-OSA Study Highlights Potential Dual-Action Benefits".
