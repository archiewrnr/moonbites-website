Restless Legs Syndrome: The Iron-and-Dopamine Story Behind the Urge to Move

Quick takeaways
  • Restless legs syndrome (RLS) is estimated to affect 5–10% of U.S. adults, per the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, and is defined less by pain than by an urge to move the legs that builds during rest and eases with movement.
  • The leading explanation isn't low iron in the blood — it's a shortage of iron specifically inside certain brain regions, where it's needed to make dopamine, which is why standard bloodwork can look normal in someone with RLS.
  • RLS becomes much more common in pregnancy: one systematic review of the third trimester found a pooled prevalence around 23%, with some individual studies reporting rates as high as 64% — and symptoms typically ease within days to weeks after delivery.

Restless legs syndrome, also called Willis-Ekbom disease, isn't simply "fidgety legs." NINDS defines it around a specific pattern: an unpleasant, hard-to-describe sensation deep in the legs paired with an almost irresistible urge to move them, both of which build up during rest and are relieved — often only temporarily — by walking or stretching. Symptoms are typically worse in the evening and at night, which is exactly when they collide with trying to fall asleep.

The mechanism researchers point to is more specific than "low iron." The idiopathic (no clear cause) form of RLS is linked to disrupted dopamine signaling, and dopamine synthesis in turn depends on adequate iron in the substantia nigra and basal ganglia, brain regions involved in movement control. Brain-imaging studies have repeatedly found reduced iron in these specific regions in people with RLS, even when a standard blood test shows their iron levels are within normal range. That mismatch — normal blood iron, low brain iron — is a big part of why RLS research shifted its focus from the bloodstream to the brain itself. A secondary form of the condition is more directly tied to iron deficiency anemia, along with pregnancy and kidney disease.

Pregnancy is one of the clearest illustrations of the iron connection. A systematic review focused specifically on the third trimester found a pooled RLS prevalence of about 23%, with some individual studies reporting rates as high as 64% in that window — far above the general population's 5–10%. Falling maternal iron and folate stores as pregnancy progresses are considered a leading driver: in one study, RLS was reported by 38.6% of anemic pregnant women, versus 17.6% of those who weren't anemic. For most people, pregnancy-related RLS fades within days to weeks of giving birth, tracking the return of normal iron status.

None of this makes RLS something to self-diagnose or self-treat with an over-the-counter fix — the underlying cause varies enough between people that it's worth a conversation with a doctor, who can check ferritin (a marker of iron stores) rather than just standard iron levels. But understanding the mechanism explains something that trips a lot of people up: why a normal blood-iron result doesn't rule RLS out, and why the condition sits at the intersection of neurology and sleep rather than being purely one or the other.

Further reading: Restless Legs Syndrome (National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke) and Prevalence of Restless Legs Syndrome in the Third Trimester of Pregnancy: A Systematic Review (PMC).
