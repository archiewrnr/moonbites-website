CBD and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2019 retrospective case series in The Permanente Journal tracked 72 adults given CBD at a psychiatric clinic; sleep scores improved within the first month for about two-thirds of those followed for poor sleep — but there was no placebo group.
  • A 2022 literature review in European Psychiatry found CBD's effect on sleep looks dose-dependent: lower doses trended more alerting, while medium-to-high doses used short-term were more likely to help people fall and stay asleep.
  • A JAMA lab analysis of CBD products sold online found roughly 70% were mislabeled — some with less CBD than claimed, some with more, and a subset containing detectable THC — a real quality-control problem in an unregulated market.

CBD, short for cannabidiol, is a non-intoxicating cannabis compound, distinct from THC, now sold widely in gummies, oils and tinctures marketed as sleep aids. Sales have outpaced the science: rigorous, placebo-controlled trials on CBD for insomnia in otherwise healthy adults remain scarce, and much of what's available comes from smaller trials, animal studies, or anxiety research that measured sleep only as a secondary outcome.

The most-cited human data comes from a 2019 case series in The Permanente Journal. Researchers reviewed charts from a psychiatric clinic where 72 adults had been given CBD alongside their usual care — 47 primarily for anxiety, 25 primarily for poor sleep. Within the first month, sleep scores improved in about two-thirds of the sleep-focused group, and CBD was generally well tolerated. But this was a retrospective, uncontrolled chart review, not a randomized trial, so it can't rule out placebo effects or the influence of the other care patients were already receiving.

A 2022 literature review pulling together animal and human studies found the picture is more complicated than "CBD makes you sleepy." Effects appear dose-dependent and even biphasic: lower doses trended mildly alerting, while medium-to-high doses used short-term were more associated with improved sleep continuity — which helps explain why anecdotal reports vary so much between products and strengths.

Separately from whether CBD works, there's the question of whether the bottle contains what the label says. A 2017 JAMA lab analysis of CBD products bought online found close to 70% were mislabeled relative to their stated CBD content, with some samples also containing detectable THC. Because CBD supplements aren't FDA-regulated like medications, that quality gap is worth weighing alongside any potency claim on a label.

Taken together: plausible, dose-dependent, and worth further study — not yet backed by the large, controlled trials that would make it a proven sleep aid. Anyone considering it, especially alongside other medications, should talk to a doctor first and look for third-party lab testing on any product.

Further reading: Cannabidiol in Anxiety and Sleep: A Large Case Series (Shannon et al., The Permanente Journal, via PMC) and Cannabidiol (CBD) and Insomnia: Literature Review (European Psychiatry, via PMC).
