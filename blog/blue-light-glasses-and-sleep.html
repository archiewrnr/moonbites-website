Do Blue-Light-Blocking Glasses Actually Help You Sleep?

Quick takeaways
  • A 2023 Cochrane review pooled 17 randomized trials (619 people) and rated the evidence on sleep as very low certainty — three trials found a sleep benefit from blue-light-filtering lenses, and three found none.
  • A crossover trial in healthy adults wearing blue-light-blocking glasses before bed found no improvement in objective sleep time or sleep quality compared with clear lenses.
  • Older, much smaller trials using tinted amber lenses in people with existing sleep complaints have reported more consistent subjective improvement — but the sample sizes are tiny and the lenses tested aren't the clear ones most consumer glasses use.

Blue-light-blocking glasses are sold as an easy fix for screen-wrecked sleep, and the basic premise isn't wrong: blue wavelengths of light suppress melatonin more than other colors, and your phone and laptop both emit plenty of it. The leap people make from there — "so wearing these glasses in the evening will help me sleep" — is where the evidence gets much shakier.

The most comprehensive look so far is a 2023 Cochrane systematic review, which pooled 17 randomized trials covering 619 people across six countries. Six of those trials specifically measured sleep outcomes, and the results split right down the middle: three reported a significant improvement in sleep scores with blue-light-filtering lenses, and three found no meaningful difference from ordinary lenses. The review's authors rated the overall certainty of the sleep evidence as very low and said they simply can't yet say whether these lenses are better, worse, or equivalent to regular glasses for sleep quality. The same review also found little evidence that the lenses reduce digital eyestrain, which is the other claim they're commonly sold on.

Separately, a randomized crossover trial in healthy adults with no diagnosed sleep problems had participants wear blue-light-blocking glasses in the hours before bed and compared that to a control condition. It found no improvement in objective measures of total sleep time or sleep quality — a notable result given that most consumer marketing targets exactly this kind of ordinary, otherwise-healthy user.

Where the picture looks slightly more promising is in smaller, older trials that tested genuinely amber- or orange-tinted lenses — which block far more blue light than the mostly-clear lenses sold today — in people who already had sleep complaints. One small randomized trial of adults wearing amber-tinted lenses for a few hours before bed found a meaningful improvement in self-reported sleep quality compared with a control lens. That's a real signal, but it came from a handful of participants, relied on subjective diaries rather than objective sleep tracking, and used lenses that filter much more aggressively than what's typically marketed as "blue-light glasses" today.

The honest summary: if blue light from screens is keeping you up, dimming your devices, lowering overall brightness, and giving yourself a screen-free wind-down window before bed have a stronger evidence base than putting on a pair of glasses. Blue-light-blocking glasses aren't harmful to try, but the current research doesn't support them as a reliable fix for poor sleep in otherwise healthy people.

Further reading: Blue-light filtering spectacle lenses for visual performance, sleep, and macular health in adults (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2023) and Amber lenses to block blue light and improve sleep: a randomized trial (PubMed).
