Daylight Saving Time and Sleep: What the Twice-a-Year Clock Change Actually Costs You

Quick takeaways
  • In January 2024, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine published an updated position statement — endorsed by more than 20 medical and civic organizations — arguing the U.S. should scrap the biannual clock change in favor of permanent standard time, not permanent daylight saving time, because standard time keeps morning light exposure better aligned with the body's natural circadian rhythm.
  • The spring transition is the harder one: Sleep Foundation research finds the average person sleeps roughly 40 minutes less than usual on the Monday after "springing forward," and some people take several days to fully re-align.
  • Older studies linking the spring change to a short-term spike in heart attacks made headlines, but a decade-long Duke Health analysis of nearly 170,000 patients — along with a more recent systematic review — found no significant increase, a reminder that early single-study findings don't always hold up.

Losing or gaining a single hour twice a year sounds trivial, but it briefly puts two internal systems out of sync: your circadian clock, which times itself to sunrise and sunset, and your social clock, which is set by whatever the wall clock says school, work, and meals start. For a few days after each switch, the two disagree, and that mismatch is what actually produces the grogginess people report — not the hour itself so much as the sudden shift in when light hits your eyes relative to your usual schedule.

That's also the reasoning behind the American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2023 position statement, updated and republished in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine in January 2024. Rather than picking a side in the "spring forward permanently" versus "fall back permanently" debate, the AASM came down clearly for keeping standard time year-round, on the basis that it places solar noon closer to clock noon and keeps morning light — the strongest cue for setting the body clock — arriving earlier in the day. Permanent daylight saving time, by contrast, would mean darker winter mornings for good, which sleep researchers argue works against how human circadian rhythms are actually built.

The spring change tends to hit harder than the fall one, since losing an hour of sleep opportunity is tougher on the body than gaining one. The Sleep Foundation cites research showing the average person's sleep duration drops by around 40 minutes the Monday after the March transition, with knock-on effects on alertness and mood lasting several days for some people. Older epidemiological studies — including work out of Sweden, Finland, and parts of the U.S. — reported short-term upticks in heart attacks and strokes in the days following the spring switch, and those findings circulated widely. But a more recent, larger analysis from Duke Health, examining a decade of hospital data on nearly 170,000 patients, found no significant increase in heart attacks tied to either transition, a result echoed by a newer systematic review and meta-analysis. The honest picture is that the cardiac risk, if it exists at all, appears far smaller and less consistent than the earlier headlines suggested.

None of this means the clock change is harmless — the sleep loss and next-day grogginess are real and well documented — but the more dramatic health claims deserve the same scrutiny as any other single study, which is exactly what the more recent research has given them.

Further reading: Permanent Standard Time Position Statement (American Academy of Sleep Medicine), Daylight Saving Time: How This Time Change Affects Sleep (Sleep Foundation) and Daylight Saving Time May Not Trigger Heart Attacks After All (Duke University School of Medicine).
