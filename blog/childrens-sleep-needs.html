How Much Sleep Do Kids Actually Need? What the Pediatric Guidelines Say

Quick takeaways
  • In 2016, a 13-member AASM consensus panel reviewed 864 published studies and set official targets: 12–16 hours for infants 4–12 months, 11–14 hours for ages 1–2, 10–13 hours for ages 3–5, 9–12 hours for ages 6–12, and 8–10 hours for teens 13–18 (all including naps where relevant).
  • A meta-analysis pooling 42 studies and 75,499 children and adolescents found short sleepers were 58% more likely to become overweight or obese than children sleeping a normal or longer duration (RR 1.58).
  • CDC analysis of national survey data found 57.8% of middle schoolers and 72.7% of high schoolers were sleeping less than their age group's recommended minimum on school nights.

Unlike most sleep advice for adults, which tends to be a range people quietly negotiate with themselves, pediatric sleep recommendations come from an actual formal process. In 2016, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine convened a 13-member panel of sleep specialists who spent ten months applying a modified RAND Appropriateness Method to 864 published studies on sleep duration and child health. The result, published in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine and endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, is the closest thing to an official number: infants 4 to 12 months need 12 to 16 hours a day including naps; toddlers 1 to 2 years need 11 to 14; preschoolers 3 to 5 need 10 to 13; school-age children 6 to 12 need 9 to 12; and teenagers 13 to 18 need 8 to 10.

The panel didn't set those ranges arbitrarily — they're tied to measurable outcomes. One of the more striking bodies of evidence concerns weight. A meta-analysis pooling 42 studies that followed 75,499 children and adolescents, with follow-up periods ranging from one to 27 years, found that children who slept less than recommended had a 58% higher relative risk of becoming overweight or obese compared with normal or longer sleepers. The mechanism isn't fully settled, but shorter sleep is consistently linked to changes in appetite-regulating hormones and more opportunity for snacking and screen time in the extra waking hours. The panel's statement also cites associations between insufficient sleep and impaired attention, behavior and learning problems, and a higher risk of injuries, hypertension and depression in children who consistently fall short.

Despite the clarity of the guidelines, most kids don't meet them. CDC analysis of Youth Risk Behavior Survey data found 57.8% of middle school students and 72.7% of high school students reported sleeping less than the recommended minimum on an average school night, with state-level rates for middle schoolers ranging from about 50% to 65%. Later survey waves suggest the shortfall among teenagers has, if anything, gotten worse rather than better. Early school start times, homework load, and evening screen use are the factors researchers point to most often — none of which have an easy fix, but all of which are at least specific enough to work with, one bedtime at a time.

Further reading: Paruthi et al., "Recommended Amount of Sleep for Pediatric Populations," American Academy of Sleep Medicine consensus statement (2016), Fatima, Doi & Mamun, "Sleep quality and obesity in young subjects: a meta-analysis," Obesity Reviews (2016), and "Short Sleep Duration Among Middle School and High School Students," CDC Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report.
