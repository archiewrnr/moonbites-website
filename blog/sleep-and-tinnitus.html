Sleep and Tinnitus: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2024 analysis of NHANES survey data found that sleep disturbance was linked to significantly higher odds of tinnitus, with the poorest sleepers showing the strongest association.
  • A separate 2024 NHANES-based study found adults sleeping less than 8 hours a night had roughly 28% higher odds of reporting bothersome tinnitus than those sleeping 8 hours or more.
  • A 2024 Oxford study in ferrets found that neural signatures of tinnitus were measurably lower during sleep than during wakefulness — early, animal-based evidence that sleep itself may temporarily quiet the phantom sound.

Tinnitus — a persistent ringing, buzzing, or hissing with no external sound source — is one of the most common reasons people report trouble sleeping, and poor sleep is one of the most common complaints among people who have it. Untangling which comes first has been difficult, but a run of 2024 research gives a clearer picture of how the two are connected.

The most direct evidence comes from a 2024 analysis published in the journal Medicine, which used NHANES survey data on US adults aged 20 to 80 collected between 2007 and 2012. It found that self-reported sleep disturbance was associated with significantly higher odds of tinnitus, and the association was strongest among people with the poorest sleep patterns overall — not just those who logged too few hours, but those whose sleep quality was worst across several measures.

A second, separate study using a different slice of NHANES data looked specifically at sleep duration and "bothersome" tinnitus — the kind that actually interferes with daily life, not just an occasional faint ring. Adults sleeping fewer than 8 hours a night had about 28% higher odds of reporting bothersome tinnitus than adults sleeping 8 hours or more. Both of these studies are cross-sectional, meaning they can show a statistical link but can't prove that short or poor sleep causes tinnitus, rather than tinnitus causing poor sleep, or both being driven by something else entirely.

A 2024 study out of Oxford offers a hint at mechanism, though with real caveats. Researchers used a ferret model of tinnitus — animals given mild noise exposure to induce the condition — and tracked neural activity across waking and sleep states. The signatures associated with tinnitus were measurably reduced during sleep, particularly deeper sleep stages, suggesting sleep may transiently dampen the abnormal brain activity behind the perceived sound. The sample was small and it's an animal study, so it's preliminary, but it builds on earlier theoretical work proposing that deep sleep could interrupt the neural loop that sustains tinnitus.

None of this proves that fixing your sleep will cure tinnitus, and the honest read is that the relationship almost certainly runs in both directions. But the consistency across very different kinds of evidence — large human survey data and controlled animal neuroscience — makes a reasonable case that protecting sleep quality is a sensible place to start if tinnitus is part of what's keeping you up.

Further reading: Association Between Sleep and Tinnitus in US Adults: Data From the NHANES 2007–2012 (Medicine, via NCBI) and Cortical Evoked Activity Is Modulated by the Sleep State in a Ferret Model of Tinnitus (PLOS ONE).
