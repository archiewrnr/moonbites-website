Saffron and Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • In a 2020 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 63 adults with self-reported poor sleep who took 14 mg of a standardized saffron extract (affron) twice daily for 28 days showed significantly greater improvements in insomnia severity and sleep quality than the placebo group.
  • A 2021 follow-up dose-response trial of 120 adults with unsatisfactory sleep, published in Sleep Medicine, found both 14 mg and 28 mg doses of the same extract taken an hour before bed improved sleep quality and next-morning mood versus placebo, and modestly raised evening melatonin levels without affecting cortisol.
  • A 2022 meta-analysis pooling multiple randomized trials found saffron supplementation associated with meaningful, if modest, improvements in sleep quality compared with placebo — while noting that most existing trials come from a small number of research groups, so larger, independently funded studies are still needed.

Saffron — the dried stigmas of Crocus sativus, famous for being the world's most expensive spice by weight — has a long history in Persian and Ayurvedic medicine as a mood-lifting remedy. Over the past decade it's picked up a second reputation in supplement aisles as a sleep aid, credited to compounds like crocin and safranal that appear to interact with serotonin signaling in the brain. The evidence base is smaller than for something like magnesium, but it's grown into a real, if narrow, body of clinical trials.

The clearest result comes from a 2020 trial led by Adrian Lopresti and colleagues, published in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. Sixty-three healthy adults aged 18 to 70 with self-reported sleep problems were randomized to take either 14 mg of a standardized saffron extract sold under the brand name affron, twice daily, or a placebo, for 28 days. The saffron group showed significantly greater improvements on the Insomnia Severity Index, the Restorative Sleep Questionnaire, and daily sleep-quality ratings than the placebo group — a reasonably comprehensive set of outcomes for a trial of this size.

A follow-up study by the same research group the next year dug into dosing and mechanism. In this trial, 120 adults with unsatisfactory sleep took either a placebo, 14 mg, or 28 mg of the extract an hour before bed for 28 days. Both saffron doses outperformed placebo on sleep quality ratings, morning mood, and a validated insomnia questionnaire — with no clear advantage to the higher dose. Notably, the researchers also measured evening melatonin and cortisol levels directly, and found saffron supplementation modestly raised melatonin without changing cortisol, suggesting a plausible biological pathway rather than just a subjective placebo-adjacent effect.

A word of caution worth keeping in view: nearly all of the strongest saffron-sleep trials to date have come from the same handful of researchers, largely using one specific patented extract at specific doses, which makes it hard to say how well the results generalize to saffron supplements generally. A 2022 meta-analysis pooling the available randomized trials did conclude that saffron supplementation is linked to real improvements in sleep quality versus placebo, which is a meaningfully positive signal — but the field is still young compared to more heavily studied options, and saffron in very high doses (well above what's used in these trials) can be toxic, particularly during pregnancy. At the modest doses studied for sleep, saffron looks like a genuinely promising, low-risk option worth watching rather than a proven fix.

Further reading: Effects of Saffron on Sleep Quality in Healthy Adults With Self-Reported Poor Sleep (Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine) and An Investigation Into an Evening Intake of a Saffron Extract on Sleep Quality, Cortisol, and Melatonin Concentrations (Sleep Medicine).
