Caffeine and Sleep: Why Genetics Decide Who Can Handle an Afternoon Coffee

Quick takeaways
  • Caffeine's effect on sleep is shaped by at least two separate genes: CYP1A2, which controls how fast the liver clears caffeine, and ADORA2A, which controls how sensitive the brain's adenosine receptors are to it.
  • A 2023 systematic review in Nutrition Reviews pooling 22 studies found CYP1A2 variation tracked most closely with caffeine's cognitive effects, while ADORA2A variation was the one linked to anxiety and sleep disturbance.
  • In a controlled study, people who identified themselves as caffeine-sensitive were more likely to carry a particular ADORA2A variant — and under caffeine before bed, their sleep EEG patterns shifted to resemble those of people with insomnia.

"Cut off caffeine by early afternoon" is standard sleep advice, and for most people it's a reasonable rule. But anyone who has watched a friend drink espresso after dinner and fall asleep within minutes — while a single 10am coffee keeps someone else awake past midnight — knows the standard advice doesn't capture the whole picture. Part of the explanation lives in two separate genes that shape caffeine's path through the body and brain in different ways.

The first is CYP1A2, the liver enzyme mainly responsible for breaking caffeine down. People vary widely in how quickly this enzyme works: some clear caffeine in a matter of hours, others take considerably longer, so the same 3pm coffee can be largely gone from a fast metabolizer's system by bedtime while still circulating in a slow metabolizer's. It's an intuitive explanation for why cutoff times differ from person to person — but a 2023 systematic review in Nutrition Reviews, which pooled evidence from 22 observational and randomized studies, found that CYP1A2 variation tracked most consistently with caffeine's effects on cognitive performance, not with sleep specifically.

The gene that review tied more directly to sleep and anxiety was ADORA2A, which codes for one of the adenosine receptors caffeine blocks to produce its alerting effect. A controlled study published in Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics found that people who already described themselves as sensitive to caffeine were more likely to carry a particular ADORA2A variant than those who didn't — and when researchers gave participants caffeine before bed, carriers of that variant showed sleep EEG patterns that shifted toward the brain-wave signatures seen in people with insomnia, even though everyone had been given the same dose.

Put together, the two genes do different jobs: one changes how long caffeine stays active in the body, the other changes how strongly the brain reacts to it while it's there. That's a fuller explanation than "fast or slow metabolizer" alone, and it fits with why two people on an identical caffeine schedule can end up with completely different nights. None of this is a case for commercial caffeine-gene testing, which the review's authors note is still ahead of what the evidence supports — but it is a reasonable case for treating your own caffeine cutoff as something to work out by paying attention to your own sleep, rather than copying a number that happens to work for someone else.

Further reading: Genetics of Caffeine and Brain-Related Outcomes: A Systematic Review of Observational Studies and Randomized Trials (Nutrition Reviews, 2023) and A Genetic Variation in the Adenosine A2A Receptor Gene (ADORA2A) Contributes to Individual Sensitivity to Caffeine Effects on Sleep (Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics, 2007).
