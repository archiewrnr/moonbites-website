Why Teenagers Can't Just "Go to Bed Earlier": The Science of School Start Times

Quick takeaways
  • A 2014 American Academy of Pediatrics policy statement explains that puberty shifts the timing of the body clock itself, delaying natural sleep onset and wake time by up to two hours compared with middle childhood — it isn't a discipline problem.
  • CDC data on nearly 40,000 U.S. public middle, high, and combined schools for the 2011–12 school year found the average start time was 8:03 a.m., and only 17.7% of schools started at 8:30 a.m. or later, the threshold the AAP recommends.
  • The AAP statement ties adequate teen sleep (8.5–9.5 hours) to lower rates of depression and obesity, fewer car crashes, and better grades — benefits tied to school start time itself, not just total hours in bed.

Every parent of a teenager has heard some version of "just go to bed earlier," and most teenagers have tried it and failed. The reason isn't willpower. Around the onset of puberty, the body's circadian clock and its overnight buildup of sleep pressure both shift later, delaying the natural time a teenager starts feeling sleepy by as much as two hours compared to when they were younger. A 14-year-old who fell asleep easily at 9 p.m. as a child may simply not feel drowsy until closer to 11, no matter how disciplined their bedtime routine is.

School schedules, meanwhile, didn't move. In its 2014 policy statement "School Start Times for Adolescents," published in Pediatrics, the American Academy of Pediatrics laid out the mismatch plainly: if adolescents' biological wake time is shifting later, an early first bell doesn't just feel unpleasant, it structurally caps how much sleep is possible on a school night. The AAP recommends middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 a.m. so students have a realistic chance at the 8.5 to 9.5 hours of sleep the group considers healthy for that age.

The scale of the mismatch shows up in the data. A CDC analysis of start times at an estimated 39,700 public middle, high, and combined schools nationwide found the average start time for the 2011–12 school year was 8:03 a.m. — and just 17.7% of schools met the AAP's 8:30 a.m. threshold. Forty-two states reported that at least three-quarters of their public schools started before 8:30. Louisiana had the earliest state average, at 7:40 a.m.; Alaska had the latest, at 8:33 a.m. In other words, for most American teenagers, the school bell rings well before their biology is ready for it.

The AAP's interest here isn't just comfort — it's connecting chronic sleep restriction in this age group to a specific list of downstream effects. Adolescents who consistently get enough sleep show lower rates of depression and being overweight, fewer motor vehicle crashes, and better grades and standardized test scores than chronically short-sleeping peers, per the policy statement's review of the evidence. None of that requires teenagers to sleep more out of virtue; it requires school start times, and the structural constraint they place on morning wake time, to stop working against a biological shift that puberty itself causes. Districts that have delayed start times have reported longer average sleep duration in exactly the way the underlying biology would predict — evidence that this is a scheduling problem with a scheduling-shaped fix, not a matter of teenagers needing to want it more.

Further reading: American Academy of Pediatrics, "School Start Times for Adolescents," Pediatrics (2014) and CDC, "School Start Times for Middle School and High School Students — United States, 2011–12 School Year," MMWR (2015).
