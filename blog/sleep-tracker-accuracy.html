Sleep Trackers: How Accurate Are They, Really?

Quick takeaways
  • A 2021 study in the journal SLEEP tested seven consumer sleep trackers against lab-grade polysomnography and found they classified sleep stages correctly only about 60–80% of the time.
  • Trackers are consistently better at estimating total sleep time than at telling light, deep and REM sleep apart, since they infer stage from movement and heart rate rather than measuring brainwaves directly.
  • An AASM-backed review put average agreement between wearables and polysomnography at roughly 75% for staging, and accuracy tends to drop further in people with diagnosed sleep disorders.

Sleep trackers have become a normal part of a lot of bedtime routines, complete with a morning "sleep score" that can feel as authoritative as a lab result. It's worth being clear about what these devices are actually measuring, and what they're guessing at.

A polysomnography study — the gold standard for measuring sleep — records brainwaves directly through EEG, along with eye movement and muscle activity, to determine exactly which stage of sleep someone is in from second to second. Consumer trackers don't have access to any of that. Most rely on an accelerometer to detect movement, plus a heart rate sensor and sometimes blood oxygen, feeding those signals into an algorithm that estimates what stage you're probably in. It's an inference, not a measurement.

That distinction shows up clearly in a 2021 study published in the journal SLEEP, in which researchers had 34 healthy adults sleep for three nights in a lab, wearing seven different consumer devices alongside full polysomnography. The trackers did reasonably well at the basic sleep-versus-wake call and at estimating total sleep time. Sleep stage classification was a different story: agreement with polysomnography landed in the 60–80% range depending on the device and the stage in question, with light and deep sleep proving harder to separate accurately than the broader sleep/wake distinction. A broader AASM-sponsored review of the wearable literature found a similar pattern, with average staging agreement around 75%.

There's also a systematic bias worth knowing about: trackers are generally better at detecting sleep than at detecting brief awakenings, since a still body reads as "asleep" even during short wake periods that an EEG would catch. That tends to make total sleep time look slightly better than it is. None of this means the numbers are useless — night-to-night trends in your own data, like a consistently later bedtime or a stretch of shorter total sleep, are still meaningful. The stage-by-stage percentages and single-night "scores," though, are best treated as a rough estimate rather than a diagnosis.

Further reading: Performance of Seven Consumer Sleep-Tracking Devices Compared With Polysomnography (SLEEP, Oxford Academic) and New Research Evaluates Accuracy of Sleep Trackers (Sleep Foundation).
