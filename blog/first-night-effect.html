The First-Night Effect: Why You Sleep Worse in an Unfamiliar Bed

Quick takeaways
  • A 2016 Brown University study published in Current Biology found that when people slept in an unfamiliar environment, one brain hemisphere — usually the left — stayed measurably more active than the other during deep, slow-wave sleep, acting like a "night watch."
  • In the same study, irregular beeping sounds aimed at the more vigilant hemisphere triggered faster waking and quicker reactions than identical sounds aimed at the more relaxed hemisphere — evidence the asymmetry has a real function, not just a measurement artifact.
  • The effect is strongest on night one and reliably fades by the second night in the same location, which is why sleep researchers and hotel-sleep guides recommend leaning on routine, familiar objects, and light/sound control rather than expecting it to disappear on its own.

Almost everyone knows the feeling: the first night in a hotel room, a new apartment, or a friend's guest bed, sleep is lighter, more fragmented, and harder to settle into than it is at home. For a long time this was written off as vague unfamiliarity or travel stress. A 2016 study out of Brown University, led by neuroscientist Masako Tamaki and published in Current Biology, found something more specific happening inside the sleeping brain itself.

Across three experiments involving 35 volunteers who slept in the lab on two separate nights a week apart, the researchers used MRI, MEG, and polysomnography to track brain activity in detail. On the first night, a network in the left hemisphere linked to the brain's default-mode system stayed noticeably more active than the equivalent network in the right hemisphere — specifically during slow-wave sleep, the deepest sleep stage. By the second night in the same room, that asymmetry had largely disappeared.

The researchers didn't stop at observing the asymmetry; they tested whether it actually did anything. While participants slept, the team played irregular beeping tones into one ear at a time. Tones aimed at stimulating the more vigilant left hemisphere (played into the right ear) were more likely to wake the sleeper, and produced a faster behavioral response upon waking, than the same tones aimed at the right hemisphere. In effect, half the sleeping brain was standing guard, monitoring for anything unusual, while the other half slept more deeply — a plausible evolutionary holdover from sleeping in genuinely unsafe, unfamiliar places.

This matters beyond trivia because it reframes bad hotel sleep as a real, measurable neurological state rather than a psychological complaint. It also explains why the fix isn't "try to relax harder": the vigilance is closer to a reflex than a mood. What does help, per sleep researchers and travel-sleep guidance, is giving the brain familiar cues to work with — keeping your usual bedtime routine, bringing a pillowcase or small object from home, using an eye mask and earplugs or white noise to blunt the exact sensory triggers the night-watch hemisphere is scanning for, and getting outdoor light soon after waking to help the second night reset faster. None of that eliminates the first-night effect, but it works with the mechanism instead of against it — and the data is consistent that by night two, in the same bed, the extra vigilance mostly stands down on its own.

Further reading: Tamaki, Bang, Watanabe & Sasaki, "Night Watch in One Brain Hemisphere during Sleep Associated with the First-Night Effect in Humans," Current Biology (2016) and Sleep Foundation, "How to Get a Good Night's Sleep in a Hotel".
