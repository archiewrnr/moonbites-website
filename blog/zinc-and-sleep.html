Zinc and Sleep: What the Evidence Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • A 2024 systematic review of eight randomized controlled trials found zinc supplementation improved subjective sleep quality scores in some groups — including older adults and ICU nurses — but had no measurable effect in others.
  • A 2022 analysis of NHANES data on more than 5,000 U.S. adults found that higher blood zinc, and a higher zinc-to-copper ratio, were linked to lower odds of unusually long sleep — though neither marker was associated with short sleep.
  • Zinc plays a real role in GABA and glutamate signaling in the brain, giving the zinc-sleep link a plausible mechanism — but researchers still call the overall evidence "inconsistent" and are calling for larger, more targeted trials.

Zinc rarely comes up in conversations about sleep the way magnesium or melatonin do, but a small, genuinely mixed body of research has looked at whether this trace mineral — best known for immune support — does anything measurable for rest. The honest summary: it might help some people a little, and nobody has fully worked out why it helps some and not others.

Zinc participates in the brain's inhibitory signaling, interacting with GABA-A receptors and acting as a natural brake on NMDA receptor activity — the same general neurological territory where several calming compounds operate. It's also a cofactor for enzymes involved in melatonin synthesis, which gives researchers a plausible biological reason zinc status might track with sleep. A plausible mechanism, though, is a long way from proof that supplementing with it changes anything.

The most direct test came in 2024, when a systematic review published in Health Science Reports pooled eight randomized controlled trials of zinc supplementation and sleep quality. In some groups — older adults taking 30 mg a day for ten weeks, dialysis patients on a similar dose, and ICU nurses given zinc sulfate for a month — zinc modestly improved scores on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index compared with placebo. In others, including women with premenstrual syndrome, children with atopic dermatitis, and adults with major depression, it made no measurable difference. Across the pooled data, the review found no consistent effect on how long people actually slept — only, in some trials, on how they rated the quality of that sleep.

A separate line of evidence looks at the zinc levels people already have rather than supplements they take. A 2022 study in Biological Trace Element Research examined blood zinc and copper in more than 5,000 adults using data from the CDC's National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, and found that higher serum zinc, and a higher zinc-to-copper ratio, were associated with lower odds of unusually long sleep — but not with short sleep. Because this is observational data, it can't say whether zinc status causes sleep differences or simply correlates with something else, like overall diet quality.

Put together, the evidence for zinc as a sleep aid is suggestive rather than settled — real signal in some trials, no effect in others, and a correlational link to sleep duration that only cuts one direction. That's a reasonable case for making sure zinc intake is adequate for reasons that have nothing to do with sleep; it's a much shakier case for taking it specifically to sleep better.

Further reading: Effects of zinc supplementation on sleep quality in humans: A systematic review of randomized controlled trials (Health Science Reports, 2024) and Associations of Serum Zinc, Copper, and Zinc/Copper Ratio with Sleep Duration in Adults (Biological Trace Element Research, 2022).
