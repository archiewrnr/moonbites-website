Night Sweats and Sleep: What Actually Causes Them

Quick takeaways
  • Night sweats have a wide range of possible causes — an overheated bedroom, menopause, certain medications, infections, and sleep disorders among them — according to the Mayo Clinic.
  • A 2022 study in Sleep and Breathing found that among sleep-clinic patients, night sweats were independently associated with a higher "hypoxic burden," a measure of how much blood oxygen drops from repeated breathing interruptions during sleep — not simply with feeling hot.
  • The Sleep Foundation reports that as many as a third of people with obstructive sleep apnea experience regular night sweats, making the combination of snoring, daytime sleepiness, and night sweats worth raising with a doctor.

Waking up drenched is more than just an uncomfortable fluke of a too-warm room — though an overheated bedroom is by far the most common explanation. The Sleep Foundation's practical advice for the occasional sweaty night is simple: keep the bedroom around 60 to 68°F, use light, layered bedding, and go easy on alcohol, caffeine, and spicy food before bed. Most one-off episodes are explained by environment and aren't something to worry about.

When sweating recurs night after night despite a cool room and light bedding, the list of possible causes gets longer. The Mayo Clinic groups persistent night sweats into a few broad categories: hormonal shifts such as the hot flashes of perimenopause and menopause, medications including certain antidepressants and hormone therapies, infections such as tuberculosis, and thyroid or other endocrine disorders. It recommends seeing a doctor when sweats are frequent, soak through sheets, or come with fever or unexplained weight loss.

One less obvious cause is sleep apnea. A 2022 study by Nigro and colleagues, published in Sleep and Breathing, examined sleep-clinic patients and found that night sweats were independently associated with a higher hypoxic burden — essentially, how much and how often blood oxygen dips during the night. In their cohort, roughly 53% of people who reported night sweats also showed significant hypoxemia, compared with 37% of those without night sweats. Frequent nightmares, restless movement, and daytime sleepiness also came out as independent predictors, suggesting that in this context, night sweats may be one visible sign of a body working overtime to compensate for disrupted breathing — not simply a reaction to heat. The authors were careful to note that an association like this doesn't prove direct cause and effect.

None of this means every warm night calls for a sleep study. But the Sleep Foundation notes that as many as a third of people with obstructive sleep apnea report regular night sweats, so when it shows up alongside loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, or persistent daytime fatigue, it's a reasonable detail to mention at a checkup — one more clue, alongside the more familiar ones, that can point a doctor toward an undiagnosed sleep disorder.

Further reading: Independent association between hypoxemia and night sweats in obstructive sleep apnea (Sleep and Breathing, 2022) and What Causes Night Sweats? (Sleep Foundation).
