Light Pollution and Sleep: What Outdoor Night Light Actually Does to Your Rest

Quick takeaways
  • A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in BMC Public Health pooled 13 studies and more than 867,000 participants and found people with the highest exposure to outdoor light at night had a 14% higher risk of obesity, and a 7% higher risk of being overweight, than those with the least.
  • A 2020 study in JAMA Psychiatry matched satellite-measured outdoor light at night to sleep data from a large sample of US adolescents; teens in the brightest-lit areas went to bed about 29 minutes later and slept roughly 11 fewer minutes on school nights than teens in the darkest areas, and also had higher rates of mood and anxiety disorders.
  • The leading explanation is circadian, not psychological: light reaching the eyes after dark suppresses melatonin and delays the body clock, whether it comes from a phone screen or a streetlight leaking in around the curtains.

Most advice about light and sleep is aimed at what happens inside the bedroom — phone screens, hallway lamps, the blue glow of a TV left on. Less discussed is the light that comes from outside: streetlights, illuminated signage, and the general skyglow of a lit-up city, often called light pollution or outdoor artificial light at night (ALAN). Unlike a phone, it isn't something you choose to look at. It's ambient, it leaks in around curtains and blinds, and these days it's measurable from satellites — which is exactly how researchers have started studying its effects at scale.

The clearest signal on long-term health comes from a 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis in BMC Public Health, which pooled 13 studies covering more than 867,000 participants. People in the highest-exposure group for outdoor light at night had a 14% higher risk of obesity than those in the lowest-exposure group, and a 7% higher risk of being overweight. Most of the underlying studies were cross-sectional, so this shows an association rather than proof that night light alone causes weight gain — but the pattern held across cohorts in both North America and Asia, and lines up with what's already known about how light exposure after dark affects melatonin and metabolic hormones.

The sleep-specific evidence is more direct. A 2020 study in JAMA Psychiatry matched satellite measurements of outdoor light at night to sleep and mental health data from a large, nationally representative sample of US adolescents. Teens living in the brightest-lit areas went to bed about 29 minutes later and slept roughly 11 fewer minutes on school nights than those in the darkest areas — and higher light exposure was also associated with higher rates of mood and anxiety disorders, independent of other factors the researchers accounted for.

The mechanism tracks with what's already understood about light and the body clock: specialized cells in the retina feed light information directly to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain's master clock, which uses that signal to time melatonin release. That system doesn't distinguish between a phone screen and a streetlight outside the window — any light reaching the eyes after dark can suppress melatonin and push the clock later. None of this means a little ambient city glow will wreck a night's sleep. But if you've already dimmed the lamps and put the phone away and still aren't sleeping well, it's worth checking what's coming in through the window, too — blackout curtains address the same exposure these studies are measuring.

Further reading: Paksarian et al., "Association of Outdoor Artificial Light at Night With Mental Disorders and Sleep Patterns Among US Adolescents," JAMA Psychiatry (2020) and Tang, Dong & Li, "Impact of Artificial Light at Night on Obesity and Overweight: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis," BMC Public Health (2026).
