Progressive Muscle Relaxation for Sleep: What the Research Actually Shows

Quick takeaways
  • Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR), developed by physician Edmund Jacobson in the early 20th century, involves deliberately tensing and then releasing each major muscle group in sequence; Harvard Health lists it as a drug-free technique commonly taught in behavioral treatment for insomnia.
  • A 1975 randomized trial published in Behavior Therapy found PMR significantly shortened people's reported time to fall asleep compared with a placebo-relaxation group and a no-treatment group — and the improvement was still present at a five-month follow-up.
  • A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials in over 1,000 perinatal women found relaxation techniques including PMR improved self-reported sleep quality and latency, though the authors called for larger, better-designed trials to confirm the effect.

Most sleep advice is about what to subtract before bed — less caffeine, less light, less scrolling. Progressive muscle relaxation is one of the few techniques built around adding something: a deliberate, physical routine you do while already lying down. It's also one of the oldest formal relaxation methods in clinical use, predating most of the supplement and sleep-hygiene research by decades.

The technique comes from physician Edmund Jacobson, who argued that unnoticed, low-grade muscle tension keeps the nervous system too activated to settle into sleep — and that systematically tensing a muscle group for several seconds, then releasing it, teaches the body to recognize and let go of that tension. Harvard Health describes the standard practice: lying comfortably, breathing slowly, then tensing and releasing muscle groups in order, typically starting at the feet and working up to the face, each round lasting a few seconds of tension followed by a longer release.

The clearest early evidence comes from a 1975 trial by Borkovec, Kaloupek, and Slama, published in Behavior Therapy. People with sleep disturbance were randomly assigned to progressive relaxation, a placebo relaxation condition, or no treatment. The PMR group reported significantly faster sleep onset than either control group, and a five-month follow-up found the gains had held — one of the first demonstrations that a purely behavioral technique could produce a durable change in how quickly people fell asleep.

More recent evidence is thinner but still points the same direction. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis in the journal Midwifery pooled 11 randomized trials covering 1,046 perinatal women and found relaxation interventions — PMR included — were linked to meaningfully better sleep quality, latency, and duration, with the self-guided, long-term practice showing the clearest benefit. The review's authors were careful to note the trials varied widely in quality and that larger, more rigorous studies are still needed before the effect can be considered settled.

None of this makes PMR a cure for insomnia, and it won't work identically for everyone. But as a free, side-effect-free technique with decades of behavioral-therapy use behind it, it's a reasonable thing to try on a night when your mind won't stop before your body does.
