The Trouble With Treating Benadryl as a Sleep Aid

Quick takeaways
  • Diphenhydramine and doxylamine — the active ingredients in Benadryl, ZzzQuil, and Unisom — are sedating antihistamines repurposed for sleep, not drugs designed for it. Mayo Clinic advises using them for only two to three nights at a time, since longer use risks rebound insomnia.
  • A 2015 JAMA Internal Medicine study of 3,434 older adults found that higher cumulative use of strong anticholinergic drugs, a class that includes diphenhydramine, was linked to a higher risk of incident dementia over an average 7.3-year follow-up — an association, not proof of cause and effect, but one the authors said appeared to persist even after people stopped taking the drugs.
  • Tolerance to the drowsy effect can build within days of nightly use, while side effects like dry mouth, constipation, and next-day grogginess don't fade at the same rate — and Mayo Clinic singles out older adults as a group that should be especially cautious.

Reach for the "nighttime" version of an allergy pill or cold medicine and you're almost certainly taking diphenhydramine or doxylamine, two first-generation antihistamines that cross into the brain and cause drowsiness as a side effect. That side effect is exactly why they got repurposed into standalone sleep aids decades ago. The problem is that nothing about how they work was designed with sleep in mind, and the research since has raised real questions about treating them as a nightly habit.

Start with how well they actually work. Mayo Clinic's guidance on over-the-counter sleep aids is blunt: these products are meant for short-term use, typically only two to three nights in a row, because tolerance to the sedating effect can build quickly, sometimes within days of continuous use. Keep taking them past that point and you risk rebound insomnia — sleep that's worse once you stop than before you started. Mayo also lists the drugs' broader anticholinergic side effects: dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, and urinary retention, with older adults facing additional risks of confusion, falls, and a faster heart rate.

The more serious open question is long-term cognitive risk. In a 2015 study published in JAMA Internal Medicine, researchers followed 3,434 adults aged 65 and older enrolled in the Adult Changes in Thought study, using pharmacy records to track their cumulative exposure to strong anticholinergic medications — a drug class that includes diphenhydramine along with certain antidepressants and bladder medications. Over a mean follow-up of 7.3 years, 797 participants developed dementia, and higher total anticholinergic exposure over the prior decade tracked with higher dementia risk. Notably, the researchers excluded the most recent year of drug use from their calculations specifically to reduce the chance that early, undiagnosed dementia was driving people toward these medications rather than the other way around — and the elevated risk still showed up, and appeared to persist even years after people stopped taking the drugs.

None of this means an occasional dose of Benadryl for a rough night is dangerous. It means these are allergy medications borrowed for a job they were never built to do long-term, and the honest research record — tolerance that builds in days, side effects that don't, and a dementia signal still being studied — is reason enough to treat them as an occasional tool rather than a routine.

Further reading: Sleep aids: Understand options sold without a prescription (Mayo Clinic) and Cumulative Use of Strong Anticholinergic Medications and Incident Dementia (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015).
